### 5.2 Project 1: CVB-P1 "LONGFLOW" – overview

CVB-P1 addresses the long-term activity of enzymes immobilised for continuous-flow manufacture. The company's aim is to develop an immobilised ω-transaminase that retains more than 80% of its activity over 500 hours of flow operation, while keeping its initial activity at or above 70% of that of the free enzyme. The target was chosen because a catalyst bed that lasts 500 hours can be replaced on a practical maintenance schedule in a continuous plant, whereas beds that last 100 to 150 hours cannot.

The transaminase used in the project is an engineered variant of a dimeric enzyme first characterised in the company's founding research. Its active site accommodates bulky ketone substrates, and it is operated with pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (PLP) as a cofactor. The enzyme is expressed in a bacterial host, purified in the company's laboratory, and immobilised on porous supports for use in packed-bed flow reactors.

The project continues feasibility work that the company carried out in the year to 31 March 2024. That work established that the engineered enzyme had the required selectivity and reasonable initial activity, but that its stability under flow was poor. The feasibility phase ended with a commercial result that was encouraging but did not meet the technical requirement for a manufacturing catalyst. The development phase, which is claimed from 1 April 2024, was designed to resolve that requirement.

### 5.3 Baseline for CVB-P1

The baseline is the state of the art at the start of the period, namely April 2024. It is the position that a competent professional in biocatalysis would have reached by applying the published knowledge and commercially available products, without the company's proprietary variant panel or support chemistry.

Commercially available immobilised transaminases are typically attached to epoxy-methacrylate resins. These supports offer high loading and good mechanical strength, and they are widely used in process development. However, under packed-bed flow at 30 to 40 °C, the immobilised enzyme loses between 40% and 70% of its activity within 100 to 150 hours. The company's own baseline measurements, described in the work log below, confirm this range. The losses arise from two mechanisms. The first is leaching of PLP, the cofactor, from its binding site. Without PLP the enzyme loses activity rapidly, and the cofactor is weakly held on the support. The second is dissociation of the dimeric enzyme into subunits, which are catalytically inactive. Covalent attachment through a single point on each monomer does not prevent this, because the attachment is not holding the dimer interface together.

Two published approaches are available to address these mechanisms. The first is multipoint attachment through a glyoxyl-agarose chemistry, which attaches the enzyme at several points and stabilises its conformation. The second is co-immobilisation of PLP on a polyethyleneimine coating, which holds the cofactor near the enzyme. These approaches are described in published literature (refs 1 to 6 in Appendix B of section 07), including work by Hartigan & Moy (2021, *J. Biocatal. Eng.*) and Ostrander et al. (2022, *Biotechnol. Bioproc. Eng.*). Both approaches improve stability, but both reduce initial activity by 50 to 60%. In addition, neither is suitable for rigid supports of the kind needed for packed-bed flow at pressures above 5 bar. Glyoxyl-agarose is a soft gel that compresses under pressure, and the polyethyleneimine coating is applied to surfaces that have insufficient pore volume for the enzyme loading needed in a commercial bed.

The baseline therefore has three features that the company's advance must address. Stability under flow must be improved to at least 500 hours. Initial activity must not fall below 70% of the free enzyme. And the solution must work on a rigid support that tolerates the pressures used in continuous manufacturing. The company's view is that no published or commercially available approach meets all three requirements, and that the problem therefore has no routine solution.

### 5.4 Advance sought for CVB-P1

The company's advance has two parts. The first is a support chemistry that is compatible with a rigid, hierarchically porous silica. The second is a surface-lysine engineering strategy applied to the enzyme. The engineering introduces a small number of surface lysine residues at positions chosen so that the enzyme can be attached to the support at several points on the surface, while the attachment points are kept away from the active site and from the dimer interface. The attachment is designed to keep the dimer cross-linked, so that dissociation is suppressed, while the cofactor is retained in situ by the pore architecture of the support.

The company did not, at the start of the period, know whether this combination would work. The advance was therefore a hypothesis, and it was tested through the sequence of experiments recorded below. The targets set at the start of the period were: retained activity above 80% after 500 hours of continuous flow at 35 °C and 5 bar; initial activity of at least 70% of the free enzyme; and enantiomeric excess of at least 99% throughout, to match the selectivity of the free enzyme.

### 5.5 Technical uncertainties for CVB-P1

The company identified three technical uncertainties at the start of the period, each of which could not be resolved by a competent professional from the published literature or from commercially available information.

**Uncertainty 1: dimer interface and active-site geometry.** Surface lysine substitutions are needed to achieve oriented, multipoint attachment. A substitution at the wrong position might destabilise the dimer interface, which would bring back the dissociation problem, or it might distort the geometry of the active site and reduce activity or selectivity. It was not known in advance which positions would be tolerated. The enzyme's structure is well characterised, but the effects of multi-site substitutions on dimer stability under immobilisation conditions could not be predicted reliably from structure alone.

**Uncertainty 2: retention of PLP without blocking the active site.** PLP must remain bound in the active site for catalysis to proceed. If it is retained by a coating or a support feature that sits too close to the active site, it might be retained but inaccessible, which would also reduce activity. If it is held too loosely, it leaches. The company did not know whether a retention strategy could achieve both properties at once.

**Uncertainty 3: pore architecture and diffusion.** A rigid, high-pressure support must have pores large enough for the substrate and product to diffuse in and out, and enough surface area for a high enzyme loading. These requirements pull in opposite directions. Large pores reduce surface area, and small pores impose diffusion limitation that reduces the apparent activity of the bed. The company did not know whether a pore architecture could be found that satisfied both.

### 5.6 Methodology for CVB-P1

The methodology was an iterative cycle of design, expression, immobilisation, characterisation and flow testing. Each cycle was recorded in LabTrack, and each result was reviewed at the weekly project meeting before the next cycle was designed.

Variants were designed by Dr Ravi Penhallow using a combination of structural analysis of the free enzyme and a lysine-mapping calculation to identify candidate positions that were solvent-accessible, distant from the active site, and not part of the dimer interface. The candidate positions were then screened by Dr Aoife Kernick against the support chemistry. Expression and purification were carried out by Tomasz Brell and Hannah Quarles, using standard His-tag affinity purification followed by size-exclusion chromatography. Protein concentration was measured by absorbance at 280 nm, and purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Dimer integrity was assessed by size-exclusion chromatography coupled to multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS). The molar mass of the eluting species was compared with the expected mass of the dimer, and a result within 5% was taken as evidence that the dimer was intact. Active-site geometry was assessed by circular dichroism spectroscopy in the far-UV, and by the specific activity of the enzyme in a pyruvate-coupled spectrophotometric assay at 30 °C with a standard bulky ketone substrate.

Stability was tested in packed-bed flow. Each support was packed into a jacketed glass column of 10 mL volume, and the substrate solution was pumped through at a fixed flow rate and temperature. Activity was measured by sampling the outlet and quantifying product by HPLC. Retained activity was expressed as a percentage of the activity at time zero. The standard test ran for 500 hours at 35 °C and 5 bar, with samples taken at 24-hour intervals and checked for enantiomeric excess by chiral HPLC.

Leon Fairhurst operated the HPLC and chiral HPLC instruments and checked the calibration of each run against a reference standard. Priya Odedra maintained the flow rigs and the cold room, and she recorded the pressure and temperature logs for each run. The laboratory's safety systems, including the fume extraction and the high-pressure interlocks, were inspected monthly.

### 5.7 Project 1 work log: April to August 2024

The work log below records each dated iteration in the order in which it occurred. The entries describe the activity, the observations, and the decision that followed. Page references are to the company's LabTrack notebook.

**3 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0412).** Project kick-off, continuing from the feasibility phase. Dr Saltash reviewed the feasibility data from the year to 31 March 2024, including the flow data from the commercial immobilised transaminase used as a baseline. The team agreed that the work would be organised around the three uncertainties in section 5.5, and that each would have a named owner. Dr Penhallow was assigned uncertainty 1, Dr Kernick uncertainty 2, and Dr Saltash uncertainty 3 with the pore architecture work.

**15 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0431 to CVB-LB-0448).** Support chemistry review. The team synthesised reference epoxy-methacrylate and glyoxyl-agarose supports for comparison with the proposed hierarchical silica route. The glyoxyl-agarose supports showed good initial loading but compressed under the test pressure, and this was consistent with the baseline. The epoxy-methacrylate supports loaded well but gave a high rate of PLP leaching, confirmed by a fall in absorbance at 412 nm in the supernatant.

**29 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0455).** Baseline packed-bed run using the commercially available immobilised transaminase on epoxy-methacrylate resin, at 35 °C and 5 bar. Retained activity fell to 47% of initial activity by 120 hours, and to 31% by 150 hours. The result sits within the 40 to 70% loss range reported in the literature, and it confirms that the commercial product cannot meet the 500-hour target. The run was repeated on a second column with the same outcome, giving 49% at 120 hours.

**13 May 2024 (CVB-LB-0478).** Surface lysine design round 1. Dr Penhallow proposed a panel of twelve variants (designated V1-01 to V1-12) carrying between one and four surface lysine substitutions. The positions were selected by the lysine-mapping calculation, with exclusion of residues within 12 Å of the active-site lysine and residues in the dimer interface as defined by the crystal structure of the free enzyme. The panel was designed to test whether the number of substitutions affected stability, and whether positions on opposite faces of the monomer behaved differently.

**27 May 2024 (CVB-LB-0502 to CVB-LB-0530).** Expression and purification of the V1 panel. All twelve variants expressed at comparable levels. Two variants, V1-07 and V1-11, showed a lower yield after affinity purification, which the team attributed to partial aggregation in the lysate. Purified protein was stored at -80 °C in a glycerol buffer.

**10 June 2024 (CVB-LB-0551).** Immobilisation of the V1 panel on the epoxy-methacrylate support, using the company's standard multipoint protocol. Ten variants immobilised without visible difficulty. V1-07 and V1-11 aggregated during immobilisation, forming visible precipitate in the coupling buffer within 40 minutes. The team recorded the result as a negative for those two variants, which indicated that the substitutions at those positions were not compatible with the coupling chemistry. Dr Kernick noted that the aggregation was consistent with exposure of a hydrophobic patch created by the substitution pair.

**24 June 2024 (CVB-LB-0573).** Stability screen of the ten variants that immobilised successfully. Each was run in packed-bed flow at 35 °C and 5 bar for 72 hours. Three variants, V1-02, V1-05 and V1-09, retained more than 75% of their initial activity at 72 hours. The remaining seven fell below 60%. The three acceptable variants were taken forward for dimer integrity testing.

**8 July 2024 (CVB-LB-0600).** Dimer integrity and active-site assessment of V1-02, V1-05 and V1-09 by SEC-MALS and circular dichroism. All three retained the dimer, with measured molar masses within 3% of the expected dimer mass. Circular dichroism spectra showed no significant change in secondary structure relative to the free enzyme. Specific activity of the free and immobilised forms was measured. V1-05 showed 66% of the free-enzyme specific activity, which was the highest of the three. V1-02 and V1-09 showed 52% and 58% respectively.

**22 July 2024 (CVB-LB-0621).** Uncertainty 2 work. Dr Kernick tested polyethyleneimine coatings on the silica prototype, which had been prepared to examine the pore architecture work in parallel. The PLP retention in the coating was good, with leaching measured at less than 4% over 96 hours in a static test. The coated support was then tested for catalysis with V1-05. Initial activity was 61% of free enzyme. The team noted that the coated surface was also affecting the active-site environment, and this was recorded for follow-up.

**5 August 2024 (CVB-LB-0640; CVB-SC-2024-08).** The polyethyleneimine co-immobilisation route was abandoned. Chiral HPLC analysis of product from the coated support showed enantiomeric excess falling from above 99% for the free enzyme to 94% for the immobilised enzyme. The loss of selectivity was reproducible across three runs. Dr Saltash concluded that the cationic coating was interacting with the substrate in the vicinity of the active site, and that the route could not be made selective without a substantial redesign. The decision was recorded in the August steering committee minutes, and the team agreed to focus effort on the support architecture.

**19 August 2024 (CVB-LB-0655).** Post-mortem and review of alternatives. The team reviewed the selectivity loss and the PLP retention data together. It was concluded that PLP retention would need to be achieved through the support geometry rather than through a surface coating. Three candidate support routes were identified for further study: a mesoporous silica series, a hierarchical silica with an interconnected macropore network, and a composite support combining a silica core with a polymer shell. Dr Kernick was tasked with a feasibility assessment of each.

The entries for April to August 2024 show the project moving from an established baseline to a set of tested hypotheses. Two of the three variant hypotheses, and the coating hypothesis for PLP retention, failed. The stability screen, the dimer work and the selectivity test each produced information that was used directly in later decisions. The team's conclusion at the end of August was that the next phase should concentrate on support architecture and on a smaller, better-characterised variant set.
