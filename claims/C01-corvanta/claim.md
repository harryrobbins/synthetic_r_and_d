### 5.2 Project 1: CVB-P1 "LONGFLOW" – overview

CVB-P1 addresses the long-term activity of enzymes immobilised for continuous-flow manufacture. The company's aim is to develop an immobilised ω-transaminase that retains more than 80% of its activity over 500 hours of flow operation, while keeping its initial activity at or above 70% of that of the free enzyme. The target was chosen because a catalyst bed that lasts 500 hours can be replaced on a practical maintenance schedule in a continuous plant, whereas beds that last 100 to 150 hours cannot.

The transaminase used in the project is an engineered variant of a dimeric enzyme first characterised in the company's founding research. Its active site accommodates bulky ketone substrates, and it is operated with pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (PLP) as a cofactor. The enzyme is expressed in a bacterial host, purified in the company's laboratory, and immobilised on porous supports for use in packed-bed flow reactors.

The project continues feasibility work that the company carried out in the year to 31 March 2024. That work established that the engineered enzyme had the required selectivity and reasonable initial activity, but that its stability under flow was poor. The feasibility phase ended with a commercial result that was encouraging but did not meet the technical requirement for a manufacturing catalyst. The development phase, which is claimed from 1 April 2024, was designed to resolve that requirement.

### 5.3 Baseline for CVB-P1

The baseline is the state of the art at the start of the period, namely April 2024. It is the position that a competent professional in biocatalysis would have reached by applying the published knowledge and commercially available products, without the company's proprietary variant panel or support chemistry.

Commercially available immobilised transaminases are typically attached to epoxy-methacrylate resins. These supports offer high loading and good mechanical strength, and they are widely used in process development. However, under packed-bed flow at 30 to 40 °C, the immobilised enzyme loses between 40% and 70% of its activity within 100 to 150 hours. The company's own baseline measurements, described in the work log below, confirm this range. The losses arise from two mechanisms. The first is leaching of PLP, the cofactor, from its binding site. Without PLP the enzyme loses activity rapidly, and the cofactor is weakly held on the support. The second is dissociation of the dimeric enzyme into subunits, which are catalytically inactive. Covalent attachment through a single point on each monomer does not prevent this, because the attachment is not holding the dimer interface together.

Two published approaches are available to address these mechanisms. The first is multipoint attachment through a glyoxyl-agarose chemistry, which attaches the enzyme at several points and stabilises its conformation. The second is co-immobilisation of PLP on a polyethyleneimine coating, which holds the cofactor near the enzyme. These approaches are described in published literature (refs 1 to 6 in Appendix B of section 07), including work by Hartigan & Moy (2021, *J. Biocatal. Eng.*) and Ostrander et al. (2022, *Biotechnol. Bioproc. Eng.*). Both approaches improve stability, but both reduce initial activity by 50 to 60%. In addition, neither is suitable for rigid supports of the kind needed for packed-bed flow at pressures above 5 bar. Glyoxyl-agarose is a soft gel that compresses under pressure, and the polyethyleneimine coating is applied to surfaces that have insufficient pore volume for the enzyme loading needed in a commercial bed.

The baseline therefore has three features that the company's advance must address. Stability under flow must be improved to at least 500 hours. Initial activity must not fall below 70% of the free enzyme. And the solution must work on a rigid support that tolerates the pressures used in continuous manufacturing. The company's view is that no published or commercially available approach meets all three requirements, and that the problem therefore has no routine solution.

### 5.4 Advance sought for CVB-P1

The company's advance has two parts. The first is a support chemistry that is compatible with a rigid, hierarchically porous silica. The second is a surface-lysine engineering strategy applied to the enzyme. The engineering introduces a small number of surface lysine residues at positions chosen so that the enzyme can be attached to the support at several points on the surface, while the attachment points are kept away from the active site and from the dimer interface. The attachment is designed to keep the dimer cross-linked, so that dissociation is suppressed, while the cofactor is retained in situ by the pore architecture of the support.

The company did not, at the start of the period, know whether this combination would work. The advance was therefore a hypothesis, and it was tested through the sequence of experiments recorded below. The targets set at the start of the period were: retained activity above 80% after 500 hours of continuous flow at 35 °C and 5 bar; initial activity of at least 70% of the free enzyme; and enantiomeric excess of at least 99% throughout, to match the selectivity of the free enzyme.

### 5.5 Technical uncertainties for CVB-P1

The company identified three technical uncertainties at the start of the period, each of which could not be resolved by a competent professional from the published literature or from commercially available information.

**Uncertainty 1: dimer interface and active-site geometry.** Surface lysine substitutions are needed to achieve oriented, multipoint attachment. A substitution at the wrong position might destabilise the dimer interface, which would bring back the dissociation problem, or it might distort the geometry of the active site and reduce activity or selectivity. It was not known in advance which positions would be tolerated. The enzyme's structure is well characterised, but the effects of multi-site substitutions on dimer stability under immobilisation conditions could not be predicted reliably from structure alone.

**Uncertainty 2: retention of PLP without blocking the active site.** PLP must remain bound in the active site for catalysis to proceed. If it is retained by a coating or a support feature that sits too close to the active site, it might be retained but inaccessible, which would also reduce activity. If it is held too loosely, it leaches. The company did not know whether a retention strategy could achieve both properties at once.

**Uncertainty 3: pore architecture and diffusion.** A rigid, high-pressure support must have pores large enough for the substrate and product to diffuse in and out, and enough surface area for a high enzyme loading. These requirements pull in opposite directions. Large pores reduce surface area, and small pores impose diffusion limitation that reduces the apparent activity of the bed. The company did not know whether a pore architecture could be found that satisfied both.

### 5.6 Methodology for CVB-P1

The methodology was an iterative cycle of design, expression, immobilisation, characterisation and flow testing. Each cycle was recorded in LabTrack, and each result was reviewed at the weekly project meeting before the next cycle was designed.

Variants were designed by Dr Ravi Penhallow using a combination of structural analysis of the free enzyme and a lysine-mapping calculation to identify candidate positions that were solvent-accessible, distant from the active site, and not part of the dimer interface. The candidate positions were then screened by Dr Aoife Kernick against the support chemistry. Expression and purification were carried out by Tomasz Brell and Hannah Quarles, using standard His-tag affinity purification followed by size-exclusion chromatography. Protein concentration was measured by absorbance at 280 nm, and purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Dimer integrity was assessed by size-exclusion chromatography coupled to multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS). The molar mass of the eluting species was compared with the expected mass of the dimer, and a result within 5% was taken as evidence that the dimer was intact. Active-site geometry was assessed by circular dichroism spectroscopy in the far-UV, and by the specific activity of the enzyme in a pyruvate-coupled spectrophotometric assay at 30 °C with a standard bulky ketone substrate.

Stability was tested in packed-bed flow. Each support was packed into a jacketed glass column of 10 mL volume, and the substrate solution was pumped through at a fixed flow rate and temperature. Activity was measured by sampling the outlet and quantifying product by HPLC. Retained activity was expressed as a percentage of the activity at time zero. The standard test ran for 500 hours at 35 °C and 5 bar, with samples taken at 24-hour intervals and checked for enantiomeric excess by chiral HPLC.

Leon Fairhurst operated the HPLC and chiral HPLC instruments and checked the calibration of each run against a reference standard. Priya Odedra maintained the flow rigs and the cold room, and she recorded the pressure and temperature logs for each run. The laboratory's safety systems, including the fume extraction and the high-pressure interlocks, were inspected monthly.

### 5.7 Project 1 work log: April to August 2024

The work log below records each dated iteration in the order in which it occurred. The entries describe the activity, the observations, and the decision that followed. Page references are to the company's LabTrack notebook.

**3 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0412).** Project kick-off, continuing from the feasibility phase. Dr Saltash reviewed the feasibility data from the year to 31 March 2024, including the flow data from the commercial immobilised transaminase used as a baseline. The team agreed that the work would be organised around the three uncertainties in section 5.5, and that each would have a named owner. **15 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0431 to CVB-LB-0448).** Support chemistry review. The team synthesised reference epoxy-methacrylate and glyoxyl-agarose supports for comparison with the proposed hierarchical silica route. The glyoxyl-agarose supports showed good initial loading but compressed under the test pressure, and this was consistent with the baseline. The epoxy-methacrylate supports loaded well but gave a high rate of PLP leaching, confirmed by a fall in absorbance at 412 nm in the supernatant.

**29 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0455).** Baseline packed-bed run using the commercially available immobilised transaminase on epoxy-methacrylate resin, at 35 °C and 5 bar. Retained activity fell to 47% of initial activity by 120 hours, and to 31% by 150 hours. The result sits within the 40 to 70% loss range reported in the literature, and it confirms that the commercial product cannot meet the 500-hour target. The run was repeated on a second column with the same outcome, giving 49% at 120 hours.

**13 May 2024 (CVB-LB-0478).** Surface lysine design round 1. Dr Penhallow proposed a panel of twelve variants (designated V1-01 to V1-12) carrying between one and four surface lysine substitutions. The positions were selected by the lysine-mapping calculation, with exclusion of residues within 12 Å of the active-site lysine and residues in the dimer interface as defined by the crystal structure of the free enzyme. The panel was designed to test whether the number of substitutions affected stability, and whether positions on opposite faces of the monomer behaved differently.

**27 May 2024 (CVB-LB-0502 to CVB-LB-0530).** Expression and purification of the V1 panel. All twelve variants expressed at comparable levels. Two variants, V1-07 and V1-11, showed a lower yield after affinity purification, which the team attributed to partial aggregation in the lysate. Purified protein was stored at -80 °C in a glycerol buffer.

**10 June 2024 (CVB-LB-0551).** Immobilisation of the V1 panel on the epoxy-methacrylate support, using the company's standard multipoint protocol. Ten variants immobilised without visible difficulty. V1-07 and V1-11 aggregated during immobilisation, forming visible precipitate in the coupling buffer within 40 minutes. The team recorded the result as a negative for those two variants, which indicated that the substitutions at those positions were not compatible with the coupling chemistry. Dr Kernick noted that the aggregation was consistent with exposure of a hydrophobic patch created by the substitution pair.

**24 June 2024 (CVB-LB-0573).** Stability screen of the ten variants that immobilised successfully. Each was run in packed-bed flow at 35 °C and 5 bar for 72 hours. Three variants, V1-02, V1-05 and V1-09, retained more than 75% of their initial activity at 72 hours. The remaining seven fell below 60%. The three acceptable variants were taken forward for dimer integrity testing.

**8 July 2024 (CVB-LB-0600).** Dimer integrity and active-site assessment of V1-02, V1-05 and V1-09 by SEC-MALS and circular dichroism. All three retained the dimer, with measured molar masses within 3% of the expected dimer mass. Circular dichroism spectra showed no significant change in secondary structure relative to the free enzyme. Specific activity of the free and immobilised forms was measured. V1-05 showed 66% of the free-enzyme specific activity, which was the highest of the three. V1-02 and V1-09 showed 52% and 58% respectively.

**22 July 2024 (CVB-LB-0621).** Uncertainty 2 work. Dr Kernick tested polyethyleneimine coatings on the silica prototype, which had been prepared to examine the pore architecture work in parallel. The PLP retention in the coating was good, with leaching measured at less than 4% over 96 hours in a static test. The coated support was then tested for catalysis with V1-05. Initial activity was 61% of free enzyme. The team noted that the coated surface was also affecting the active-site environment, and this was recorded for follow-up.

**5 August 2024 (CVB-LB-0640; CVB-SC-2024-08).** The polyethyleneimine co-immobilisation route was abandoned. Chiral HPLC analysis of product from the coated support showed enantiomeric excess falling from above 99% for the free enzyme to 94% for the immobilised enzyme. The loss of selectivity was reproducible across three runs. Dr Saltash concluded that the cationic coating was interacting with the substrate in the vicinity of the active site, and that the route could not be made selective without a substantial redesign. The decision was recorded in the August steering committee minutes, and the team agreed to focus effort on the support architecture.

**19 August 2024 (CVB-LB-0655).** Post-mortem and review of alternatives. The team reviewed the selectivity loss and the PLP retention data together. It was concluded that PLP retention would need to be achieved through the support geometry rather than through a surface coating. Three candidate support routes were identified for further study: a mesoporous silica series, a hierarchical silica with an interconnected macropore network, and a composite support combining a silica core with a polymer shell. Dr Kernick was tasked with a feasibility assessment of each.

The entries for April to August 2024 show the project moving from an established baseline to a set of tested hypotheses. Two of the three variant hypotheses, and the coating hypothesis for PLP retention, failed. The stability screen, the dimer work and the selectivity test each produced information that was used directly in later decisions. The team's conclusion at the end of August was that the next phase should concentrate on support architecture and on a smaller, better-characterised variant set.

### 5.8 Project 1 work log: September 2024 to March 2025

The work log continues from the August 2024 review. In September the team moved from the abandoned coating route to a structured support-architecture study, and from there to a second variant panel designed around the support that had been selected.

**9 September 2024 (CVB-LB-0690).** Support screen of the mesoporous silica series. Dr Kernick prepared three silica supports with nominal pore diameters of 12, 20 and 30 nm, all of which were functionalised with the same amine-reactive linker used in the epoxy-methacrylate baseline. Enzyme loading was measured by protein assay of the supernatant before and after coupling. The 12 nm support carried 19 mg of enzyme per gram, which was too low for the target bed density. The 20 nm and 30 nm supports carried 28 and 31 mg per gram. Under the 5 bar test, the 30 nm support showed compression of the pore network, with a measured pressure drop increase of 37% over 24 hours, and the 20 nm support showed a smaller increase of 9%. The team concluded that mesoporous silica could reach adequate stability at 20 nm, but that enzyme loading would remain below the level needed for a commercially sized bed.

**23 September 2024 (CVB-LB-0712).** First test of the hierarchical silica prototype. The prototype combined mesopores of approximately 15 nm for enzyme loading with an interconnected macropore network of approximately 800 nm for mass transfer and for pressure tolerance. Enzyme loading was 45 mg per gram, the highest yet measured on a rigid support. The variant V1-05 was immobilised on the prototype and run at 35 °C and 5 bar. Initial activity was 64% of free enzyme and retained activity at 72 hours was 79%. The macropore network prevented the pressure build-up observed on the mesoporous supports. The team recorded a clear signal that the architecture had potential, but a 72-hour test is not long enough to judge stability against the 500-hour target.

**14 October 2024 (CVB-LB-0740; CVB-SC-2024-10).** Decision to adopt hierarchical silica as the primary support for CVB-P1. The steering committee reviewed the September results alongside the August selectivity data. The composite silica-polymer shell route was deprioritised because its enzyme loading was lower than that of the hierarchical prototype and because it required an additional coating step that raised the risk of selectivity loss. The polyethyleneimine route was formally closed. The committee approved a second variant panel designed specifically for the hierarchical silica support, and it approved a dedicated flow rig for the work.

**28 October 2024 (CVB-LB-0758).** Diffusion study on the hierarchical silica support. A bed of V1-05 was loaded at four levels, from 15 to 40 mg of enzyme per millilitre of bed volume, and each bed was run at 6 bar. The results showed that initial activity increased with loading up to 25 mg per millilitre, and then levelled off. Above that loading the apparent activity of the bed fell, and the team attributed the fall to diffusion limitation within the mesopores at the bed scale. The design target for enzyme loading was therefore set at 25 mg per millilitre of bed, which is below the maximum possible loading but above the level needed for a commercially sized bed.

**11 November 2024 (CVB-LB-0779).** Uncertainty 2 work on the hierarchical silica support. Two strategies for PLP retention were compared. In the first, PLP was fed continuously with the substrate. In the second, PLP was loaded onto the support before enzyme immobilisation, so that it was held within the pore network close to the immobilised enzyme. Continuous feeding gave retained activity of 66% at 96 hours and consumed PLP at a rate that was not acceptable for a commercial process. Pre-loading gave a PLP leaching rate of 2% over 96 hours in a static test, and retained activity of 74% at 96 hours in flow. Chiral HPLC confirmed enantiomeric excess of 99.3%, which matched the free enzyme. The pre-loading strategy was adopted for the second variant panel.

**2 December 2024 (CVB-LB-0801).** Surface lysine design round 2. The second variant panel, V2, was designed using the results of the first panel and the hierarchical silica support. Dr Penhallow kept the lysine positions that had been tolerated in V1-02, V1-05 and V1-09, and excluded the positions associated with aggregation in V1-07 and V1-11. The panel contained eight variants, V2-01 to V2-08, each with two to four surface lysine substitutions. The panel was designed to test the combined effect of the best-tolerated positions and the new support architecture.

**9 December 2024 (CVB-LB-0814).** Immobilisation of the V2 panel on the hierarchical silica support using the pre-loaded PLP protocol. Seven variants immobilised without visible aggregation. V2-06 aggregated during the coupling step and was dropped from the panel. Initial activity of the seven was measured against the free enzyme. V2-04, which carries four surface lysine substitutions at positions on the monomer face opposite the active site, gave 61% of free-enzyme activity. V2-01 gave 55%, and the remaining five variants gave between 38% and 52%.

**16 December 2024 (CVB-LB-0826).** Structural checks on V2-04. SEC-MALS confirmed that the immobilised dimer was intact, with measured molar mass within 3% of the expected dimer mass. Circular dichroism of the free enzyme showed no significant change from the parent enzyme. A 96-hour screen in flow gave retained activity of 88% at 96 hours on the hierarchical silica support, the best result yet at that time point. The team agreed that V2-04 would be the lead variant for the long-run test, subject to improving its initial activity.

**13 January 2025 (CVB-LB-0838).** Optimisation of the immobilisation protocol for V2-04. ### 5.9 Project 1 outcomes

The outcomes of CVB-P1 in the period are as follows.

First, the company achieved its primary technical target on a laboratory-scale packed bed. The engineered transaminase immobilised on hierarchical silica retained 85% of its activity at 500 hours and 83% at 612 hours, at 35 °C and 5 bar. The baseline reported in the literature had lost 40 to 70% of activity by 150 hours. The measured result therefore exceeds the baseline on stability by a wide margin.

The company's view is that the scale-up uncertainty will require further investigation, and it is planned for the financial year ending 31 March 2026.

### 5.11 Project 2: CVB-P2 "PREDICTA" – overview

CVB-P2 addresses a different problem from CVB-P1. Where CVB-P1 seeks a more stable immobilised enzyme, CVB-P2 seeks a way of predicting, before any wet-laboratory work is done, how much activity a given enzyme variant will retain after covalent multipoint attachment. The company's variant panels can contain hundreds of candidates. Each candidate must currently be expressed, purified, immobilised and tested in flow, which takes several weeks per panel. A validated predictor would allow the panel to be pruned before the laboratory work begins, and it would concentrate the company's laboratory capacity on the variants most likely to succeed.

The project is distinct from the computational work that the company routinely carries out in support of its enzyme engineering, which uses structure prediction and docking to propose candidate mutations. Those tools predict the structure of the free enzyme. They do not predict the activity retained after immobilisation, and the company's experience is that the two are only loosely correlated. The company's dataset of immobilisation experiments is therefore the essential resource for CVB-P2, and no equivalent public dataset of comparable size and consistency was available at the start of the period.

### 5.12 Baseline for CVB-P2

At the start of the period, structure-prediction and docking tools could predict the three-dimensional structure of a free enzyme with good accuracy, and they could estimate the binding energy of a substrate or cofactor in the active site. Those capabilities are well established. There was no validated method, in the published literature or in commercial software, for predicting the fraction of activity an enzyme retains after covalent immobilisation on a porous support. The effects of immobilisation depend on the position of the attachment points relative to the dimer interface, the flexibility of the surface, the local chemistry of the support, and the coupled effects of those factors under flow. None of these is captured by a free-enzyme structure.

In practice, the field screens empirically. A candidate panel is immobilised and tested, and the best performers are taken forward. The company's own feasibility work for CVB-P1 followed this approach, and it confirmed that a panel of twelve variants, which takes approximately six weeks to express, immobilise and test, can yield a single useful lead. The baseline for CVB-P2 is therefore an empirical screening process that is correct in its outcome but slow and expensive, and that offers no predictive guidance before the experiments are run.

### 5.13 Advance, uncertainties and methodology for CVB-P2

The advance sought is a surrogate model trained on the company's dataset of 340 immobilisation experiments, with inputs that describe the free-enzyme structure, the proposed attachment points, and the surface accessibility of those points computed by coarse-grained molecular dynamics. The company's hypothesis is that accessibility, which is the extent to which an attachment point and its neighbourhood are exposed to the support and to the solvent during flow, carries information about retained activity that the free-enzyme structure does not. The target was a Spearman rank correlation coefficient above 0.6 between predicted and measured retained activity on held-out variants. Rank correlation was chosen because the company's use of the model is to rank candidates for pruning, and an absolute error measure would have been less relevant to that use.

Two uncertainties were identified at the start of the period. The first was whether a coarse-grained surface-accessibility signal would carry enough information to predict retained activity, given that coarse-grained models necessarily omit fine atomic detail. The second was whether the dataset of 340 experiments was large enough to train a model that generalised to new variants, or whether the model would learn the particular features of the company's own variant families and fail on new families. Neither uncertainty could be resolved by a competent professional from the published literature, since the required combination of data and modelling had not been reported.

The dataset was assembled from the company's laboratory records for the years to 31 March 2024 and for the period. Each record gives the variant identity, the attachment chemistry, the bed configuration, the initial activity and the retained activity at fixed time points. The records were standardised in April 2024 by Dr Kernick and Dr Penhallow, and each was checked against the original laboratory page. Records with missing or inconsistent fields were excluded, and the final dataset of 340 experiments was locked for training on 30 April 2024.

The modelling methodology has three components. The first is a feature set. Structure-derived features were computed from the free-enzyme structure, including solvent-accessible surface area, local flexibility from the crystallographic B-factors, and distance to the dimer interface. The second is a coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulation of each variant, run on the company's GPU compute, from which a surface-accessibility feature was extracted for each attachment point. The third is a surrogate model, a Gaussian process regressor with an automatic relevance determination kernel, which was chosen because it provides uncertainty estimates alongside its predictions and because the dataset was small. A gradient-boosted tree model was used as a comparator.

Evaluation was carried out by five-fold cross-validation during development, and then by a held-out set of variants that were withheld from training until the model was frozen. The held-out set was designed to contain variant families not represented in the training set, so that the second uncertainty could be tested directly.

### 5.14 Project 2 work log

**1 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0418).** Dataset assembly began. The company's immobilisation records for the years to 31 March 2024 were gathered and transcribed into a common format. The initial assembly contained 287 experiments. Dr Kernick recorded in the page that a further 53 experiments from the period would be added as they were completed.

**22 April 2024 (CVB-LB-0447).** Feature set design. Dr Penhallow proposed structure-derived features for each variant and attachment point, and the team agreed the list. The list was reviewed against the physical reasoning for each feature, so that no feature was included solely because it improved a fit. Solvent-accessible surface area and distance to the dimer interface were retained. Crystallographic B-factors were retained as a proxy for flexibility. Eleven further features were considered and rejected for lack of physical motivation.

**20 May 2024 (CVB-LB-0493).** First model, using structure-derived features only. Five-fold cross-validation gave a Spearman correlation of 0.21. The result was consistent with the expectation that the free-enzyme structure alone would be an incomplete predictor. The team recorded that the structure-only model was a baseline for the subsequent work and that a correlation of 0.21 was not useful for pruning.

**17 June 2024 (CVB-LB-0566; CVB-CM-2024-06).** Coarse-grained molecular dynamics set up for sixty variants, selected to span the range of attachment chemistries and dimer positions in the dataset. Simulations were run on the company's GPU compute in batches, with each run logged in the compute record. The first batch of twelve variants was completed by the end of the month, and the accessibility feature was extracted for each attachment point.

**15 July 2024 (CVB-LB-0609).** Surface-accessibility feature added to the model for the sixty variants with simulation data. Five-fold cross-validation gave a Spearman correlation of 0.41 on that subset. The improvement over the structure-only model was taken as evidence that accessibility carries information about retained activity. The team noted that the improvement was substantial but that the subset was small, and that the model had not yet been tested on the full dataset.

**12 September 2024 (CVB-LB-0703).** Extension of the coarse-grained runs to all variants with attachment-point data. The dataset was examined for coverage in lysine-substitution space. The team found that coverage was sparse for variants with three or more substitutions, and that most variants in the dataset came from families sharing a common substitution pattern. This finding was relevant to the second uncertainty, and the team recorded that the held-out evaluation would need to include variants from different families. Cross-validation on the full dataset gave a Spearman correlation of 0.44 with the Gaussian process model.

**14 October 2024 (CVB-LB-0741).** Gaussian process model adjusted for the change in support architecture from mesoporous silica and epoxy-methacrylate to hierarchical silica. The architecture was encoded as a categorical feature. Cross-validation performance was unchanged at 0.44, and the team concluded that the architecture feature was not the limiting factor. The result was discussed with the CVB-P1 team, who agreed to record support architecture for all future experiments so that the dataset could be extended consistently.

**10 December 2024 (CVB-LB-0822).** First held-out evaluation. Forty variants from families not represented in the training set were withheld from training, and the model was frozen on the remaining 300 experiments. The Spearman correlation on the held-out variants was 0.47. The team recorded that this was below the target of 0.6 and that the second uncertainty might be limiting. The model's predictive intervals were checked and found to be wider for the held-out families, which the team regarded as a sign that the model was correctly reporting its lower confidence.

**6 January 2025 (CVB-LB-0834).** Recalibration of the coarse-grained simulations, using a revised set of solvent parameters that the team had reviewed against the literature. Simulations for the held-out variants were repeated under the revised parameters. The Spearman correlation on the held-out set rose to 0.49. The team concluded that the improvement was small but genuine, and that the coarse-grained signal was sensitive to parameter choice in a way that required further work.

**14 February 2025 (CVB-LB-0872).** Final held-out evaluation for the period. A second held-out set of 52 variants was assembled, including the V2 panel variants immobilised in December 2024 for CVB-P1, which were the most recent data and were not in the training set. The model was frozen before evaluation. The Spearman correlation on this set was 0.52. The result was below the target of 0.6. The team noted that the model's ranking was useful for pruning, since the top quartile of predictions contained the majority of the variants with retained activity above 80% at 96 hours, but that the model did not meet the target for rank correlation across the full set.

**28 March 2025 (CVB-LB-0908).** Project review. Dr Saltash reviewed the two uncertainties against the outcome of the period. The coarse-grained accessibility signal was judged to carry useful information, because it improved the model in every evaluation in which it was tested. The dataset size was judged not yet sufficient to resolve the second uncertainty. The team agreed that the project would continue into the following period, with two actions: the dataset would be extended with the flow data from CVB-P1, and the held-out evaluation would be repeated on a larger set of families. The project status was recorded as partly successful.
