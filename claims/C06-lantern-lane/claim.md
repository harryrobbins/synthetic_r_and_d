### 5.3 Project 1: Development of an innovative concealed-hinge bespoke larder system

#### 5.3.1 Field and context

Project 1 concerns the design of a bespoke larder unit. A larder is a full-height cabinet, usually located in a kitchen or utility room, that stores food and kitchen equipment. In the Company's bespoke work, larders are normally built with exposed or semi-concealed hinges and a fixed internal shelf layout, which the Company's joiners have refined over many years.

The project's question was how a larder could be built with a new internal layout and with concealed hinges that would allow the door to close flush with the carcass, while still meeting the Company's standards for structure, function and finish.

#### 5.3.2 Baseline

The baseline for Project 1 is traditional joinery practice for bespoke larder units. This practice uses established carcass construction, with dadoed or dowelled shelves, conventional butt hinges or rebated hinges and standard shelf spacings. The Company has built larders to this practice for more than a decade and regards it as the baseline against which the new design is judged.

#### 5.3.3 Advance sought

The advance sought was a larder unit with a new internal layout and a concealed hinge arrangement. The Company's aim was to provide greater storage access, with pull-out and adjustable internal fittings, and a cleaner external appearance with no visible hinge. The advance is an improvement on the traditional joinery baseline in that it solves the technical problem of integrating a commercially available concealed hinge into a bespoke carcass without loss of structural performance or finish quality. A competent joiner working from traditional practice would not be able to resolve this question without building and testing prototypes, because the interaction between hinge geometry, carcass thickness, door weight and the required reveal depends on the particular materials and tolerances used.

#### 5.3.4 Uncertainties

The uncertainties for Project 1 were as follows.

- Whether the design would meet customer expectations for access and appearance.
- Whether the hinge arrangement could carry the loads expected of a full-height larder door over a long period of use.
- Achieving a premium finish within budget.
- Minimising material waste in the carcass and door components.

The first uncertainty was the principal one. The Company could not determine in advance whether a larder with the proposed layout would be accepted by clients as an improvement on the existing product, or whether the new arrangement would produce doors that sat unevenly or sagged. The second uncertainty concerned the structural behaviour of the hinge and carcass combination. The Company could not calculate the expected performance from first principles, because the manufacturer's data assumed a standard carcass that was not representative of the Company's bespoke construction.

#### 5.3.5 Methodology

The Company's methodology was to build a series of prototype larder units, each incorporating one or more design changes, and to test each unit for fit, function and finish. The prototypes were built in the workshop using the Company's standard equipment, together with the CNC router for the carcass panels from December 2024. Each prototype was assessed against the success criteria set in advance. The criteria were:

- door alignment within a reveal tolerance of 1.5 millimetres across the full height of the unit;
- a door that opens and closes through 110 degrees without binding after 5,000 cycles on the test rig;
- a finish that passes the Company's visual inspection standard at the first and second coat;
- no more than a defined allowance of offcut waste per unit.

The prototypes were tested in the workshop and then installed in two client homes for a trial period. The client homes were chosen because they represented typical kitchen layouts, and the clients agreed to provide feedback on the operation of the doors and the internal fittings.

#### 5.3.6 Work log

- **3 June 2024.** Project opened. Craig Lantern set out the design brief and the success criteria. Initial sketches prepared, with three alternative internal layouts.
- **18 June 2024.** Iteration 1. Prototype carcass built in mixed oak and MDF to test the proposed hinge position. Door alignment measured at 4.1 millimetres at the top of the unit. Result recorded as unsatisfactory against the 1.5 millimetre criterion.
- **2 July 2024.** Iteration 2. Carcass modified to include a stiffening rail and revised hinge spacing. Alignment improved to 2.6 millimetres. Result recorded as partially satisfactory.
- **24 July 2024.** Iteration 3. Hinge type changed to a concealed hinge with a wider mounting plate. Alignment measured at 1.4 millimetres. Criterion met on the test unit.
- **15 August 2024.** Iteration 4. Full-height prototype built in walnut veneer ply with oak frame. Door opened and closed on the rig for 1,200 cycles without binding.
- **9 September 2024.** Iteration 5. Internal layout revised to include pull-out fittings and adjustable shelves. Fit tested with a sample set of crockery and small appliances.
- **21 October 2024.** Iteration 6. Finish trials carried out on the door faces. First coat showed minor blistering at the edge joints. Edge treatment revised.
- **11 November 2024.** Iteration 7. Second finish trial. Blistering eliminated. Finish assessed as meeting the visual inspection standard at the second coat.
- **9 December 2024.** Iteration 8. Carcass panels cut on the CNC router for the first time within this project. Panel accuracy compared with hand-cut panels from iteration 4. Prototype rebuilt using machined panels.

#### 5.3.7 Outcomes

The outcome of Project 1 was a larder design with a concealed hinge arrangement and a new internal layout. The design meets the Company's success criteria for alignment, cycle performance and finish. The door alignment tolerance was achieved on the production prototype, the cycle test was passed and the finish met the visual inspection standard.

The design was not adopted in the form tested at iteration 1 or 2. The Company's experience of those iterations was that the hinge position and carcass stiffness were critical to the outcome, and this knowledge has been incorporated into the Company's documented larder specification. The Company now offers the concealed-hinge larder as a standard option for bespoke work.

Prototypes were iteratively developed, tested against defined success criteria, and refined based on observed performance. The result is a design that a competent joiner could not have produced from traditional practice without the testing described above.

### 5.4 Project 2: Advanced CNC toolpath optimisation for hardwood components

#### 5.4.1 Field and context

Project 2 concerns the use of a computer-controlled router to machine hardwood components for bespoke furniture. The Company bought the router in August 2024 to replace a proportion of its hand-cut and router-table work. The machine is a Bramwell 2030 Pro, which is supplied with a standard set of toolpath strategies for softwood and engineered board.

The question was how to generate toolpaths that give consistent results on oak and walnut, which have different grain behaviour, density and tendency to tear out, so that the machine could produce bespoke parts to a repeatable standard.

#### 5.4.2 Baseline

The baseline for Project 2 is traditional joinery practice for hardwood components, in which parts are shaped by hand and with conventional machine tools. Where machine tools are used, the Company relies on standard toolpath settings and on the operator's judgement to adjust feed rates, depths of cut and passes. The Company's joiners regard this as the established method for hardwood work.

#### 5.4.3 Advance sought

The advance sought was a toolpath strategy for hardwood components that gives a consistent surface finish and tolerance across oak and walnut, without repeated hand correction. The Company's aim was to reduce the time spent correcting parts by hand and to allow a joiner to set up a new component without a long process of trial and error. The advance is an improvement on the baseline in that it establishes a documented and repeatable process for hardwood machining on this machine, which the standard software settings do not provide.

#### 5.4.4 Uncertainties

- Whether the toolpaths could achieve the required surface quality on hardwood.
- Whether tool wear could be controlled across a production run.
- Achieving the required tolerances without excessive machining time.
- Minimising the waste of hardwood during set-up and test cutting.

The principal uncertainty was the relationship between feed rate, spindle speed, depth of cut and the grain direction of each hardwood. The Company could not predict from the software documentation which combination would give a clean cut in oak and in walnut, and the supplier's settings were calibrated for softer materials.

#### 5.4.5 Methodology

The Company's methodology was a series of test cutting runs on offcut hardwood, each run using a defined toolpath strategy and parameter set. Each run was measured for surface quality, dimensional accuracy and tool wear. Results were compared against the Company's tolerance specification of 0.3 millimetres on critical dimensions and against a visual standard for surface finish. The parameter set that met both criteria was then used to cut a sample component, which was assembled and checked against the bespoke part specification.

#### 5.4.6 Work log

- **12 August 2024.** Router installed in the machining bay. Operator training provided by the supplier's engineer. Standard toolpaths trialled on pine offcuts.
- **29 August 2024.** Iteration 1. Standard oak toolpath run at manufacturer's default settings. Surface tear-out observed on the grain-reversing sections. Result recorded as unsatisfactory.
- **16 September 2024.** Iteration 2. Feed rate reduced and a second finishing pass added. Tear-out reduced but not eliminated. Dimensional accuracy within 0.5 millimetres.
- **7 October 2024.** Iteration 3. Toolpath changed to climb cutting on the finishing pass. Surface quality improved on oak. Walnut showed burning at the slower feed rate.
- **28 October 2024.** Iteration 4. Separate parameter sets for oak and walnut established. Walnut spindle speed raised and depth of cut reduced. Walnut burning eliminated.
- **18 November 2024.** Iteration 5. Tool wear measured after 40 metres of cutting. Wear within the supplier's tolerance but dimensional drift observed at the end of the run.
- **13 December 2024.** Iteration 6. Coolant-free tool change interval adopted. Dimensional drift eliminated across a 60-metre run.
- **22 January 2025.** Iteration 7. Full sample component cut in oak and walnut. Parts assembled and checked against the bespoke part specification. Both parts met the tolerance on critical dimensions.
- **18 February 2025.** Iteration 8. Hardwood waste measured across a set-up cycle. Offcut use reviewed and set-up procedure revised to reduce test cutting.
- **14 March 2025.** Toolpath strategy documented in the workshop procedure file and adopted for production work.
- **28 March 2025.** Project closed for the purposes of this claim.

#### 5.4.7 Outcomes

The outcome of Project 2 was a documented toolpath strategy for oak and walnut components, with separate parameter sets, a tool change interval and a set-up procedure. The strategy meets the Company's tolerance specification on critical dimensions and produces a surface finish that passes the visual standard. Set-up time for a new hardwood component has been reduced, although the Company has not measured the reduction against a formal baseline.

Prototypes were iteratively developed, tested against defined success criteria, and refined based on observed performance. The strategy was not available from the supplier's documentation, and it could not have been derived from the baseline of traditional joinery practice, because that practice did not involve machine toolpaths for hardwood.

#### 5.4.8 Boundaries

Project 2 started on 12 August 2024, when the router was installed and the first standard toolpaths were trialled. It ended on 28 March 2025, when the toolpath strategy was documented and the project was closed for the purposes of this claim.

#### 5.4.9 Competent professional statement

Craig Lantern is the competent professional for Project 2. His experience of hardwood joinery, including the behaviour of oak and walnut under cutting, was used to interpret the results of each iteration and to select the parameter sets for each species. The competent professional could not have determined the relationship between machine settings and hardwood behaviour from the supplier's documentation, because that documentation did not address hardwoods.

### 5.5 Project 3: Sustainable finishing techniques

#### 5.5.1 Field and context

Project 3 concerns the evaluation of finishing systems for bespoke timber work. The Company's finishing process has traditionally used solvent-based lacquers and oil finishes applied by hand in a ventilated booth. The Company wished to reduce the environmental impact of its finishing without reducing the appearance or durability that clients expect.

The project's question was whether a commercially available water-based lacquer could be applied to bespoke work to the required standard, in the Company's booth and on the timbers used in its kitchens and furniture.

#### 5.5.2 Baseline

The baseline for Project 3 is traditional joinery finishing practice, using solvent-based lacquers and oil finishes applied by hand, as widely used in the bespoke joinery trade. The Company's practice is to apply two or three coats, with sanding between coats, and to assess the finish visually.

#### 5.5.3 Advance sought

The advance sought was a finishing technique that uses a water-based lacquer and provides a finish that is comparable in appearance and durability to the traditional system. The Company's aim was to establish whether the water-based system could be used on bespoke work, in the Company's own process and conditions, without a loss of quality. The advance is an improvement on the baseline because it reduces the solvent content of the finishing process while maintaining the standard of work clients expect.

#### 5.5.4 Uncertainties

- Whether the water-based lacquer would achieve a finish that clients would regard as premium.
- Whether the finish would perform in domestic kitchens subject to heat and moisture.
- Achieving the finish within budget, given the cost of the new material.
- Minimising material waste during application and sanding.

The principal uncertainty was whether the water-based lacquer would perform in the conditions of a domestic kitchen. Solvent-based lacquers are known to cure hard and to resist water. The Company could not determine from the manufacturer's data sheet whether the water-based system would achieve the same resistance on the Company's timbers, which include oak with a high tannin content.

#### 5.5.5 Methodology

The Company's methodology was a series of finishing trials on sample boards in oak, walnut and MDF, followed by trials on completed client pieces where the client agreed. Each trial was assessed for appearance, hardness and water resistance. Hardness was assessed by pencil and scratch test. Water resistance was assessed by a standing test with a damp cloth and a ring test with a warm cup. Appearance was assessed visually against the Company's standard sample board.

#### 5.5.6 Work log

- **9 September 2024.** Project opened. Water-based lacquer obtained for trial. Sample boards prepared in oak, walnut and MDF.
- **30 September 2024.** Iteration 1. First coat applied to sample boards. Surface raised slightly on the oak boards and the finish appeared cloudy.
- **21 October 2024.** Iteration 2. Sanding sequence revised and a sealing coat added. Cloudiness eliminated on walnut. Oak still slightly raised.
- **12 November 2024.** Iteration 3. Oak boards conditioned before finishing. Surface raise reduced to an acceptable level. Visual assessment against standard board recorded as satisfactory on walnut and MDF.
- **4 December 2024.** Iteration 4. Hardness and water resistance tests carried out. Scratch resistance judged lower than the solvent baseline. Water resistance judged satisfactory after a standing test of 30 minutes.
- **15 January 2025.** Iteration 5. Additional coat added. Hardness improved to the standard required for kitchen work, as judged by the Company's test.
- **10 February 2025.** Iteration 6. Trial on a completed client piece, a dresser in walnut, with the client's agreement. Client reported satisfaction with the appearance after a fortnight of use.
- **17 March 2025.** Water-based system adopted for standard work after the completion of the final trial.
- **21 March 2025.** Project closed for the purposes of this claim.

#### 5.5.7 Outcomes

The outcome of Project 3 was an assessed finishing system that uses a water-based lacquer on oak, walnut and MDF, with a defined sanding sequence, a sealing coat and a conditioning step for oak. The system meets the Company's visual standard and passes the water resistance test. Its scratch resistance is lower than the solvent-based baseline, and the Company has documented this in its finishing procedure.

Prototypes were iteratively developed, tested against defined success criteria, and refined based on observed performance. The finishing system is now the Company's standard for much of its bespoke work, and the Company's solvent use has been reduced accordingly.

#### 5.5.8 Boundaries

Project 3 started on 9 September 2024, when the water-based lacquer was obtained and the first sample boards were prepared. It ended on 21 March 2025, when the finishing system was adopted and the project was closed for the purposes of this claim.

#### 5.5.9 Competent professional statement

Craig Lantern is the competent professional for Project 3. His experience of bespoke finishing, including the behaviour of oak and walnut under lacquer and the performance of finishes in domestic kitchens, was used to set the test criteria and to judge the results. The competent professional could not have determined from the trade literature alone whether the water-based system would perform on the Company's timbers in its own conditions.

### 5.6 Overall conclusion

The Company's three projects addressed questions that a competent professional in joinery could not resolve from existing practice. Each project followed a defined methodology, recorded its work contemporaneously and reached an outcome that was documented in the Company's procedures. The directors confirm that the work described in this section was carried out by the Company in the accounting period from 1 April 2024 to 31 March 2025, and that the start and end boundaries given for each project are correct.
