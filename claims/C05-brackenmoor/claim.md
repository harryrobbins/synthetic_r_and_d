### 5.3 Project P1: Hallam Road deep basement

**Project title:** Hallam Road deep basement: innovative retaining solution in challenging ground conditions.

**Site description.** The project site is a Victorian terrace on Hallam Road, Sheffield. The client's brief was to construct a two-storey basement beneath the existing house, providing habitable space at lower ground level. The house forms part of a continuous terrace, and the party walls on both sides carry the load of the adjoining houses on shallow foundations. Excavation to the proposed formation level would take the base of the new basement well below the foundations of the party walls.

**Ground conditions.** The desk study identified a thin layer of made ground over glacial deposits, with a high water table close to the existing ground level. Historic records indicated former industrial use on the adjacent plot, but the extent and character of any made ground beneath the party walls could not be confirmed from the information available before the works were let. The ground conditions beneath and around the excavation were therefore unknown in their detail at the time of design.

**Client contract.** The works were let under a JCT design-and-build variant on a fixed-price basis of £1.38 million. The client's design team appointed the structural and temporary works designers. The Company was responsible for the construction of the basement, including the temporary works, the retaining structure as built, the monitoring arrangements during construction and the coordination of the construction programme.

The design of the retaining solution was carried out by Pennine Structural Consultants, who were appointed by the client's design team at RIBA Stage 3 and novated to the Company under the design-and-build contract; their fees, which were recovered within the fixed contract sum, are included in the subcontract costs at Section 6. The design-development outputs were reviewed by the Company's site engineering team, which then developed the detailed temporary works sequence and the monitoring arrangements that are the subject of this claim.

### 5.4 Project P1: Baseline

The baseline for P1 was the Company's established practice for basement retention in comparable urban terraced sites, together with the general industry practice understood by experienced groundworks teams. In the Company's experience, retaining walls in terraced housing are commonly formed using contiguous or secant piles, with a temporary propping sequence installed as excavation proceeds, and with ground and building movement monitored against trigger levels.

That practice was not sufficient, on its own, to give confidence in the performance of the wall at Hallam Road. The combination of a high water table, made ground of uncertain character, and a party wall with a shallow foundation meant that the response of the ground and the adjacent structure to staged excavation could not be predicted with confidence from existing practice. The baseline therefore provided the starting point for the investigation, but did not resolve the questions on which the claim is based.

### 5.5 Project P1: Technological advance sought

The Company sought to advance its enhanced capability in deep basement construction. Specifically, it sought:

(a) a retaining solution for a two-storey basement beneath a Victorian terrace, formed within a high water table and made ground, that could be built without unacceptable movement of the adjacent party wall;

(b) a temporary propping and excavation sequence, tied to measured movement, that could be applied to comparable basements in similar ground, and that the Company could specify and control on site without reliance on repeated external redesign; and

(c) a monitoring and decision process that allowed excavation to be released stage by stage against defined criteria, so that the Company could demonstrate the performance of the work to the client, the party wall surveyor and the building control authority.

The advance was not a matter of commercial preference. It was a question of whether the Company's approach to deep basement construction could be extended into more demanding ground than it had previously worked in, and whether the result could be recorded in a form that would allow the Company to repeat it.

### 5.6 Project P1: Technological uncertainties

Three uncertainties were identified before the work began.

**Uncertainty 1: stability of the adjacent party wall during excavation.** The party wall carried the load of the adjoining house on a shallow foundation. Excavation to formation depth would reduce the lateral support available to the wall. The degree of movement that the wall could accommodate, and the point at which that movement would become unacceptable, could not be readily deduced from the desk study or from the design information available at the time.

**Uncertainty 2: unknown ground conditions.** The made ground beneath and around the excavation was of uncertain extent and character. Its stiffness, permeability and response to dewatering could not be confirmed until the piles were installed and the first excavation stage was taken down. The presence of obstructions, including old foundations and services, was also unknown.

**Uncertainty 3: sequencing of propping in a high water table.** The sequence of excavation and propping needed to control both ground movement and groundwater inflow. The number of propping levels, their timing and the extent of dewatering required were not known in advance and could not be determined by a standard method without monitoring data from the site.

Each uncertainty was identified in writing before the work started and was recorded in the design-development log, with a corresponding monitoring or investigation response.

### 5.7 Project P1: Systematic approach adopted

The Company adopted a staged approach, in which each design or temporary works decision was set out as a hypothesis, tested by measurement on site, and then either confirmed or revised. The approach was applied in the following way.

First, before piling began, the Company installed an array of inclinometers in the ground beside the party wall and a set of groundwater standpipes within the excavation footprint. Baseline readings were taken over a period of several weeks, so that the normal pattern of movement and groundwater level was known before any load was changed.

Second, the Company set amber and red monitoring triggers for lateral movement and for vertical movement of the party wall. The triggers were agreed with the client's design team and the party wall surveyor. The triggers were set so that an amber reading required the site team to review the temporary works before the next stage, and a red reading required the work to stop until the designer had confirmed the response.

Third, each excavation stage and each propping level was treated as an iteration. Designs and temporary works were iteratively developed, tested against defined success criteria, and refined based on observed performance. The success criteria were that movement of the party wall remained within the amber trigger, that groundwater inflow was controlled within the dewatering arrangement, and that each propping level was installed in the sequence set out in the method statement.

Fourth, every iteration was recorded in the project log, with the date, the readings, the decision taken and the person responsible. Site photographs were taken at each stage, and requests for information (RFIs) were raised whenever the design or the ground differed from the assumptions in the current method statement. The RFI register and the monitoring log are listed in the evidence index at Section 7.

Fifth, the Company reviewed the results of each stage before releasing the next. This review was carried out by the site engineering team with the Contracts Manager, and where the designer's input was needed, it was obtained before the next stage proceeded.

All activities were undertaken in a systematic, iterative and controlled manner, with outcomes recorded contemporaneously.

**Outcomes.** The basement was completed within the accounting period, with the party wall remaining within the amber trigger throughout the works. The peak lateral movement recorded in the monitoring log was 9 mm at inclinometer I4 in January 2025, within the amber trigger of 10 mm. The Company has recorded how the secant pile wall, the propping sequence and the dewatering arrangement behaved in made ground with a high water table, and how each responded when the ground conditions differed from the assumptions in the design.

Three outcomes are of particular significance to the advance. First, the Company established a monitoring-based release process for each excavation stage, which allowed the works to be controlled against measured performance rather than against fixed programme dates. Second, the Company identified the sensitivity of the party wall to lift height, and set a reduced lift height where the readings required it. Third, the Company identified a localised permeable pocket in the made ground, which the designer's revised wellpoint layout addressed.

The outcomes have been incorporated into the Company's temporary works procedures and are now applied to comparable basement projects.

**Start boundary.** The P1 work within the claim began on 1 August 2024, with the site mobilisation and the pre-start recording of the party wall. The design-development review, the monitoring installation and the baseline readings all fall within the accounting period.

**End boundary.** The P1 work within the claim ended on 25 July 2025, with the completion of the basement and the final monitoring readings. Subsequent maintenance and defects work is not included in this claim.

## 5. Technical Narrative: Project P2, Competent Professional and Overall Approach

### 5.11 Project P2: Digital transformation of site operations

**Project title:** Digital transformation of site operations.

**Background.** By the start of the accounting period the Company's site information was held in several forms. Site supervisors kept paper diaries, which were transcribed to the office at the end of each week. Photographic records were held on individual phones and were not consistently filed against the relevant project or activity. Survey information for groundworks, including levels, excavation volumes and drainage runs, was exchanged as PDF drawings and spreadsheets, and was frequently out of date by the time it reached the site.

The Company had identified these weaknesses across its live contracts. Its management team wished to integrate site diary entries, drone survey data and building information model (BIM) views into one site information flow, so that a supervisor on site could see the current state of the works and the most recent survey, and the office could see the same information without transcription.

**Baseline.** The baseline was the Company's paper-based diary and photographic practice, together with the manual survey workflow commonly used by groundworks contractors of its size at the time. The commercially available tools for construction site diaries, drone capture and BIM viewing were understood by the Company's management, but no established method existed for integrating them on groundworks sites with limited connectivity and a workforce with varied digital experience. That integration was the subject of the work.

**Technological advance sought.** The Company sought to advance its capability to manage site information digitally by integrating three existing technologies, namely a cloud-based site diary application, drone survey capture and a BIM viewer, into one site information flow. The aim was an improvement in the accuracy, timeliness and retrievability of site records across concurrent projects, and a method for configuring and training site teams that could be repeated on future contracts.

**Technological uncertainties.** Two uncertainties were identified.

The first was whether site teams would adopt the technology in daily use. Site operatives work outdoors in varied conditions, share devices and in some cases have limited experience of digital tools. Whether a supervisor and a group of operatives would consistently record diary entries, photographs and inspections in the new system could not be known from the Company's previous experience.

The second was whether the integrated data flow would function reliably on remote sites where connectivity was intermittent, and whether drone survey outputs could be converted into BIM viewer data without manual rework. This question was technical and could not be answered from supplier documentation alone.

**Systematic approach adopted.** Designs and workflows were iteratively developed, tested against defined success criteria, and refined based on observed performance. The success criteria were defined before the pilot began. They were that at least 80 per cent of operatives on a pilot site recorded diary entries in the system for each working week, that drone survey outputs could be viewed in the BIM viewer within one working day of capture, and that the diary remained usable offline for at least one working day.

Pilot use was carried out on Hallam Road and on two further live sites. Adoption and performance were reviewed at fixed intervals, and changes to the configuration, the training material and the offline arrangements were recorded in the project log. Each change was tested against the success criteria at the next review.

**Work log.** The principal iterations of the P2 work were as follows.

- 2 September 2024. Requirements were set out by the management team. The three existing technologies were shortlisted, and a set of success criteria was agreed in writing.
- 23 September 2024. The cloud site diary application was selected. Configuration of the project structure for the Hallam Road pilot began, with activity codes matched to the Company's existing cost codes.
- 14 October 2024. Pilot use began on Hallam Road. Supervisors recorded diary entries on mobile devices, and the first review found that operatives were recording entries less consistently than the success criteria required. The configuration was simplified and a short-form entry was introduced.
- 4 November 2024. The second review found that offline use was not reliable on the basement site, where connectivity was poor. The application's offline mode was tested and the sync settings were changed. Photographs were set to upload at the end of each shift rather than on capture.
- 18 November 2024. Drone survey capture trial. Two surveys were flown over the Hallam Road site and a third over a second live site. The output files were converted and loaded into the BIM viewer. The conversion took more than a working day on the first attempt and was revised in the following week's trial.
- 2 December 2024. The conversion workflow was revised so that survey outputs were processed overnight. The BIM viewer was tested against the surveyed levels for the excavation, and the difference between the survey and the model was recorded for each level.
- 13 January 2025. Training was delivered to site supervisors and to a group of operatives on the pilot site. Training material was revised after the first session, and a short visual guide for operatives was produced in response to the feedback recorded.
- 3 February 2025. Pilot review. Adoption was recorded at 68 per cent of operatives on the pilot site, below the success criterion. The training approach was revised to include a supervised first entry each day, and the review criteria were reset for the following fortnight.
- 17 March 2025. Second pilot review. Adoption was recorded at 84 per cent of operatives on the pilot site, meeting the success criterion for the first time. Offline use was confirmed for a full working day on the basement site.
- 14 April 2025. The configuration was extended to the two further live sites, with the revised training and offline arrangements applied from the start.
- 9 June 2025. Adoption review across the three sites. Diary entries were recorded in the system for the majority of working days on each site, and the BIM viewer was in routine use by supervisors and the estimating team.
- 25 July 2025. P2 activities within the accounting period were concluded. The configuration, training material and offline arrangements were recorded for reuse on the Company's future contracts.

**Outcomes.** The Company's site teams adopted the integrated system at a level sufficient for continued use on all live sites. The configuration and training approach that achieved adoption are documented, and the Company has a tested basis for site information management on future projects. The drone survey to BIM conversion now completes within one working day, and offline operation of the diary has been confirmed on a site with poor connectivity.

**Boundaries.** The P2 work within the claim began on 2 September 2024, when the requirements were set and the success criteria were agreed. It ended on 25 July 2025. Licence fees for the commercial applications, which were paid under standard subscription terms, are not claimed as qualifying expenditure in this claim.

**Competent professional.** The P2 work was directed by Darren Kettlewell, with input from Paul Kettlewell on the programme of site use and from Amira Saleh on the survey and BIM workflow. Darren Kettlewell's qualifications and experience are set out at Section 5.8. His experience of supervising site operatives and of the practical limits of digital tools on groundworks sites allowed him to judge whether the pilot results met the success criteria and what the next iteration should address.

### 5.12 Overall statement on the Company's approach

The Company's approach across both projects was consistent. Each uncertainty was identified in writing before the work began, with the baseline against which the work was to be judged recorded alongside it. Each iteration was tested against defined criteria, and each outcome was recorded at the time it was reached rather than reconstructed afterwards. Decisions were taken by people with the knowledge and experience to judge them, and where the judgement required input from a designer or a specialist supplier, that input was obtained before the relevant work proceeded.

The Company does not regard the work as routine construction, nor as a continuation of its existing practice. In each project, the outcome was not known in advance, and the way in which the outcome was reached is recorded in this narrative so that a competent professional reading it could follow the reasoning, reproduce the approach and check the result.

Throughout the claim period the Company sought to achieve an appreciable improvement in the overall knowledge and capability in its field, resolving uncertainties that could not be readily deduced by a competent professional working in the field, in accordance with paragraphs 3, 6 and 13 of the DSIT Guidelines.

All activities were undertaken in a systematic, iterative and controlled manner, with outcomes recorded contemporaneously.
