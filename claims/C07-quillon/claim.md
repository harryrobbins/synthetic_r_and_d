### 3.7 Project 1: QH-LOWIR

**Title.** Iridium loading below 0.2 mg/cm² at the anode with degradation below 10 µV/h at 2 A/cm².

**Main field of science or technology.** Electrochemistry, specifically the catalysis and durability of proton exchange membrane water electrolysis.

**Baseline.** Commercial PEM anodes use between 1 and 2 mg/cm² of iridium. Published low-loading approaches, including iridium on doped titanium dioxide supports and thin-film nanostructured catalysts, have shown degradation above 30 µV/h under dynamic load because of support dissolution and loss of contact with the porous transport layer. A competent professional in the field would not have expected a low-loading anode to meet the Company's combined loading and durability target without new work.

**Advance sought.** A graded catalyst layer on a coated porous transport layer, with interface engineering that holds contact resistance stable over the lifetime of the stack. The advance is in the combination of loading, durability and dynamic operation, which the baseline does not achieve.

**Uncertainties.** The dissolution mechanism under the load-cycling profiles typical of renewable electricity supply. Whether the porous transport layer coating survives long-duration operation. The interaction between the ionomer and the catalyst at low loading.

**How and when resolved.** Through 36 membrane electrode assembly builds, approximately 14,000 cumulative test hours, and accelerated stress testing at an independent laboratory in the Netherlands, because the Company's bespoke protocol required a multi-cell rig available at the required scale and with immediate availability, which UK alternatives could not provide without a six-month delay to the programme. A passivation failure in the titanium transport layer was identified in May 2025 from post-test surface analysis and addressed by a niobium-doped coating in September 2025. Durability at 0.18 mg/cm² over 2,000 hours was measured at 6.8 µV/h by December 2025. Durability over the full target stack life is not yet established, and the work continues under the same project.

**Principal technical outputs.** A graded catalyst layer at loadings below 0.2 mg/cm², a coated porous transport layer that resists passivation at the outer step, an ionomer formulation (IF-4) with adequate adhesion at low loading, and a coating process specification for production-scale transport layers.

### 3.8 Project 2: QH-DYNA

**Title.** Stack-level thermal management for dynamic operation.

**Main field of science or technology.** Chemical and process engineering, specifically heat transfer and thermal control in electrolyser stacks.

**Baseline.** Electrolyser stacks are normally designed for steady operation at a fixed current. Thermal control strategies developed for steady operation do not respond adequately when current is changed rapidly, which causes local temperature excursions and uneven membrane hydration.

**Advance sought.** A stack-level thermal management approach that maintains temperature uniformity across the active area during rapid current changes without a significant increase in parasitic load.

**Uncertainties.** The thermal response of the stack under the fastest current ramps seen in renewable supply. The effect of local temperature excursions on membrane life. The trade-off between coolant flow and parasitic power.

**Principal technical outputs.** A validated stack thermal model, a predictive coolant flow plan (CP-4) and a local temperature threshold for membrane fatigue specific to the Company's stack design.

### 5.2 Background to electrolyser catalysis

A proton exchange membrane electrolyser splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using an electric current. The membrane conducts protons from the anode to the cathode. At the anode, water is oxidised to oxygen, a reaction that is kinetically slow and that requires a catalyst. Iridium oxide is the catalyst of choice for PEM anodes because it combines reasonable activity with resistance to the strongly oxidising, acidic conditions in the anode. It is also among the scarcest of the platinum-group metals, and its supply is concentrated in a small number of producers.

The anode catalyst layer is deposited on a porous transport layer (PTL), which carries current from the bipolar plate to the membrane and carries water and oxygen away from the catalyst. The PTL is typically a sintered titanium material. Under the oxidising conditions at the anode, titanium is prone to forming a passive oxide layer that increases the electrical resistance at the PTL interface. Maintaining low and stable contact resistance between the PTL and the catalyst layer is therefore one of the central problems of PEM electrolyser design.

The catalyst layer is bonded to the membrane and combined with the cathode in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA). The ionomer, a perfluorosulfonic acid polymer, binds the catalyst particles together and provides the proton pathway within the catalyst layer. The ionomer and the catalyst interact at the surface of the iridium particles, and this interaction governs both the proton conductivity of the layer and the accessibility of the catalyst to the electrolyte.

### 5.3 Baseline

The commercial baseline for PEM anodes is well established. Commercial stacks currently use anode iridium loadings of between 1 and 2 mg/cm². At these loadings the catalyst layer is thick enough to provide a durable electrical and proton pathway, and degradation under dynamic load is adequately controlled for the operating life that commercial customers specify.

The published research baseline for low-loading anodes is less settled. Two families of approach have been reported in the literature. The first places iridium on doped titanium dioxide supports, which allows a much smaller quantity of iridium to be dispersed over a larger surface area. The second uses thin-film nanostructured catalysts, in which the iridium is deposited as a very thin, high-area structure. Both approaches reduce the iridium loading substantially. However, in the published accounts available to the Company at the start of 2025, both showed degradation rates above 30 µV/h under dynamic load. The degradation was attributed in the literature to two mechanisms: dissolution of the support material, and loss of contact between the catalyst layer and the PTL.

The Company therefore identified the baseline for QH-LOWIR as follows. A commercially acceptable anode is one with an iridium loading of 1 to 2 mg/cm² and a degradation rate that is low enough to meet the customer's warranty over the stack life. A low-loading anode published in the research literature has an iridium loading below 0.2 mg/cm² but has degradation above 30 µV/h under dynamic load. No published approach combined a loading below 0.2 mg/cm² with degradation below 10 µV/h at 2 A/cm² under dynamic conditions.

The Company's own preliminary work in 2024 confirmed the picture in the published accounts. Its first low-loading samples, built with a commercial support, showed degradation above the published threshold within the first several hundred hours of test. The Company therefore concluded at the start of 2025 that the target combination could not be reached by combining existing approaches, and that a new approach was required.

### 5.4 Advance sought

The advance sought by QH-LOWIR is a graded catalyst layer deposited on a coated porous transport layer, with interface engineering that keeps the contact resistance between the PTL and the catalyst layer stable over the operating life of the stack.

The graded layer varies the iridium loading and the ionomer content through the thickness of the catalyst layer. The loading is highest at the interface with the membrane, where the proton pathway and the electrochemical activity are concentrated, and lowest at the interface with the PTL, where the requirement is for electrical contact and mechanical stability. The intention is that the total loading is kept below 0.2 mg/cm² while the activity and durability of the layer are maintained.

The coated PTL is the second element of the advance. The Company has developed a coating for the titanium transport layer that is intended to prevent passive oxide formation at the PTL interface while remaining electrically conductive. The coating is applied before the catalyst layer is deposited and is designed to keep the interface resistance stable through the lifetime of the stack.

The combination is the advance. Neither the graded layer nor the coated PTL alone is novel in the literature in general terms. The advance lies in combining them at low loading so that the durability target is met under dynamic load, which the baseline shows is not achieved by the approaches that are published.

### 5.5 Scientific and technological uncertainties

At the start of 2025 the Company identified three principal uncertainties that could not be resolved from the published literature or from the Company's existing knowledge.

**Uncertainty 1: the dissolution mechanism under renewable load profiles.** The published degradation mechanisms were established under constant or step-change load. Renewable electricity supply produces load profiles that vary continuously, with rapid transients and periods at low current. The Company did not know whether the dissolution mechanism that governs support degradation under constant load would dominate under these profiles, or whether a different mechanism would take over. The answer determines which material properties must be controlled to meet the durability target.

**Uncertainty 2: survival of the PTL coating.** The coating that the Company proposed to apply to the PTL had not been tested over long durations at the current densities of interest. It was not known whether the coating would survive the combined effects of oxidation, mechanical stress from compression and gas evolution, and the electrochemical potentials at the anode. A coating that delaminated or cracked would expose the titanium and allow the passive layer to form.

**Uncertainty 3: ionomer-catalyst interaction at low loading.** At higher loadings, the ionomer can be distributed through the catalyst layer in a manner that is relatively insensitive to the detailed arrangement of catalyst particles. At low loading, the catalyst particles are sparser and the ionomer must bind to a smaller surface. It was not known whether the ionomer formulations used at higher loadings would provide adequate proton conductivity and adhesion at 0.2 mg/cm² or below, or whether the ionomer would need to be reformulated, and if so, how the reformulation would interact with the graded structure.

These uncertainties were recorded in the Company's project plan dated 6 January 2025, which is available in the evidence index at Appendix 7.2.

### 5.6 Methodology

The Company adopted a build-test-analyse methodology. Each iteration of the design was implemented as a numbered MEA build, from MEA-012 onwards for the period, with each build recorded in the laboratory notebook and assigned a unique identifier. Each build was tested in a single-cell test station under a defined protocol, and the results were analysed to decide the next iteration.

The test protocol had three elements. The first was a conditioning and polarisation sequence, used to establish the baseline electrochemical performance of each build at the start of test. The second was a dynamic load profile derived from the renewable supply data described in section 5.5, applied at 2 A/cm² for periods of up to 2,000 hours. The third was a set of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements taken at regular intervals to track changes in the contact and charge transfer resistances. Degradation was measured as the rate of voltage increase at constant current, expressed in µV/h, after the first 100 hours of operation to remove the initial conditioning effect.

Surface and structural characterisation was carried out on selected builds at the start and end of test. The University of Tyneford provided XPS analysis of the catalyst surface, which identifies the oxidation state of the iridium and the chemical state of the support and coating. The Company's own scanning electron microscope was used for cross-section imaging of the catalyst layer and the PTL interface.

Accelerated stress tests (AST) were used to explore the failure modes more rapidly than the standard protocol allowed. The AST protocol applied repeated potential cycles, with high upper potentials, to the MEA, and was designed to drive support dissolution and PTL passivation in a time-compressed manner. The AST protocol was specific to the Company's cell geometry and to the targeted loading, and is described in more detail in section 5.12.

Each iteration was judged against three criteria: the degradation rate at 2 A/cm², the contact resistance measured by impedance, and the post-test characterisation of the catalyst layer. An iteration was taken forward only if it improved one of these criteria without degrading another. Iterations that failed were analysed to identify the cause of failure before the next build was designed.

### 5.7 Iterative design approach

The graded catalyst layer was developed through a series of variations on the layer structure: the number of grading steps, the loading at each step, the ionomer content at each step, and the method of deposition. The coated PTL was developed in parallel through variations of the coating composition, the coating thickness and the coating process. Two streams of work were run together, with the results of each stream feeding into the design of the other, because the performance of the graded layer depends on the properties of the PTL interface, and the interface properties depend on the catalyst layer that is deposited on them.

Ionomer formulations were developed by Dr Sun-Hee Park in parallel with the layer structure work. Each formulation was assessed for its proton conductivity and adhesion at the loading of the build in which it was used. Reformulations were introduced where the earlier formulation showed poor adhesion at low loading, and the effect of each reformulation on the durability of the layer was assessed in the following builds.

Dr Joel Abernethy led the electrochemical characterisation. His analysis of the polarisation and impedance data for each build formed the basis for the decision on the next iteration, and the interpretation of the dynamic test data was carried out under his direction and reviewed by the competent professional.

### 5.8 Use of the University of Tyneford

The University of Tyneford provided XPS analysis on a fee basis for selected MEA builds. The analysis was used to determine the oxidation state of the iridium at the surface of the catalyst layer after test, and to confirm whether the coating on the PTL remained intact. The analysis results were used directly in the interpretation of the failure modes described in section 5.10. The University had no role in the design of the MEAs or in the interpretation of the claim, and is not connected to the Company.

#### 5.10.1 January 2025

#### 5.10.2 February 2025

The dynamic load emulator at the Tyneford stack test facility was commissioned on 3 February. The emulator reproduces the current profile recorded from the Company's pilot customer's renewable supply, scaled to the single-cell active area. From this date all dynamic tests on QH-LOWIR used the emulator in preference to the earlier step-change profile, which had been used in 2024 and which the Company considered to under-represent the transients of renewable supply (QHL-LB-0424).

MEA-014 was built on 6 February using the three-step layer from MEA-013 on a PTL with a first-generation coating. The coating was a thin conductive oxide film. The degradation rate under the emulator profile was 8.9 µV/h at a total loading of 0.27 mg/cm². This was the first build of the period to fall below the 10 µV/h threshold, although the loading remained above the 0.2 mg/cm² target. The result was attributed to the coating, which reduced the rise in contact resistance seen in MEA-012 and MEA-013 (QHL-TD-0427).

Between 12 and 20 February the Company built MEA-015 and MEA-016 to test the ionomer. Dr Sun-Hee Park found that IF-3 showed adhesion loss at loadings below 0.25 mg/cm², visible in the cross-section images from the scanning electron microscope as a separation between the catalyst layer and the membrane at the outer step. A reformulation designated IF-4 was prepared, with a higher proportion of the sulfonated backbone (QHL-LB-0431, QHL-LB-0436).

#### 5.10.3 March 2025

MEA-017, built on 4 March, was the first build with IF-4. The loading was 0.22 mg/cm². The adhesion problem was no longer visible in the cross-section, and the degradation rate was 7.7 µV/h over 600 hours. The proton resistance at 2 A/cm² was 12% lower than in MEA-014. The result was reported to the Board at its meeting of 11 March, where the Company's position was recorded that the ionomer reformulation was a material step in the advance (QHL-MIN-0311).

Between 17 and 28 March the Company built MEA-018 and MEA-019 on a second-generation coated PTL. This coating was a thicker film with a higher proportion of conductive dopant. Both builds were tested for 500 hours. ### 5.12 Accelerated stress testing and the external laboratory

#### 5.12.1 Purpose of the AST campaign

The Company's long-duration tests take up to 2,000 hours to show a meaningful degradation trend, and the failure modes that matter for the target life of a stack, such as support dissolution and slow passivation, can take many thousands of hours to appear under normal operation. Accelerated stress testing is the standard means of compressing this timescale. The AST protocol applies repeated potential cycles, with high upper potentials, to the MEA. The conditions drive support dissolution and PTL passivation in a time-compressed manner, so that the Company can assess whether a candidate layer will survive the target life before committing to the full test.

The Company's AST protocol was developed in 2024 and was specific to the Company's cell geometry, the PTL area used in its MEAs and the iridium loadings targeted by QH-LOWIR. Its design was based on the published AST literature for low-loading anodes, adapted for the Company's layer structure and for the dynamic profile used in the emulator tests.

#### 5.12.2 Choice of laboratory

The AST campaign was carried out by Stichting Waterstof Testlab, an independent foundation based in the Netherlands (the "Dutch laboratory"). The Dutch laboratory was selected because it is the only facility able to run the Company's bespoke AST protocol at the required scale with immediate availability. The protocol requires a multi-cell test rig able to hold eight MEAs under potential cycling at the same time, with independent control of each cell and continuous logging of potential and current. The Company's enquiries in 2024 identified no UK facility with a rig of this specification that could take the work in the period. Two UK laboratories were approached. Both offered to build a comparable rig, but each estimated a lead time of around six months, which would have delayed the programme by six months and would have pushed the test of the coated PTL beyond the period in which the Company's pilot customers had scheduled their stack evaluations.

The Dutch laboratory offered immediate availability, a rig already configured for the Company's protocol with minimal adaptation, and a schedule that allowed the first campaign to begin in late July 2025. The Company's view, supported by its technical advisers, was that the value of the AST results depended on their timing as much as on their quality, and that the choice of the Dutch laboratory was the correct technical and commercial decision for the programme.

#### 5.12.3 AST campaign and results

The Company dispatched coated PTLs and matched catalyst-coated membranes to the Dutch laboratory on 21 July 2025. The first campaign ran from 28 July to 12 September 2025 and tested eight MEAs, comprising the Nb-doped coating in its first and second forms and the Nb-v3 coating. The campaign report, reference QHL-AST-2025-01, is dated 26 September 2025 and is included in the evidence index.

The campaign showed that the first and second forms of the coating were not sufficient for the outer step, consistent with the in-house results described in section 5.11.3. The Nb-v3 coating survived the AST protocol with no delamination and no passive oxide formation at the outer step, which supported the decision to take Nb-v3 forward into long-duration testing. A second campaign on the final loading range began on 3 November and is reported in QHL-AST-2025-02, which was received on 15 December 2025.

The laboratory's services were invoiced at £120,000 for the campaigns in the period, reference INV-SWT-2025-0031 and INV-SWT-2025-0047. The Company's cost claim for the laboratory is set out in section 6.

#### 5.12.4 Relationship to the claim

The AST work was a direct part of the investigation of the failure modes of QH-LOWIR, and was carried out under the direction of Dr Nadia Fenwick and Dr Joel Abernethy. The Company's staff specified each protocol, assessed each result and decided the design of each subsequent build. The Dutch laboratory provided the test environment and the raw data, and the Company's staff performed the interpretation.

### 5.15 Project 2: QH-DYNA, stack-level thermal management for dynamic operation

#### 5.15.1 Overview

QH-DYNA addresses the thermal behaviour of a full electrolyser stack when its current is changed rapidly. It is a smaller project than QH-LOWIR and draws on a smaller part of the team's time. It is, however, a separate project with its own baseline, advance and uncertainties, and its work was carried out in a separate test programme on the Company's stack test benches.

The project is a consequence of the same operating requirement that drives QH-LOWIR. Electrolysers paired with renewable generation must follow the output of wind and solar plant, which can change current by large amounts within minutes. The stack's thermal behaviour under these conditions determines the membrane hydration, the local temperature at the catalyst layer and therefore the rate at which the layer degrades. QH-LOWIR's durability results depend on the thermal boundary that the stack provides, and QH-DYNA was established to address that boundary at stack level.

#### 5.15.2 Baseline

Electrolyser stacks are normally designed and controlled for steady operation at a fixed current. The thermal management strategy for a stack is typically based on a coolant flow rate set to the mean heat load, with a control loop that responds to the outlet temperature. Such strategies have been shown in the Company's own earlier work, and in the published operating experience of stack manufacturers, to be adequate for steady operation.

Under rapid current changes, the same strategies do not respond adequately. The outlet temperature lags the local temperature in the stack, so that the control loop reacts after the local temperature has already risen or fallen. The published accounts of such operation describe local temperature excursions of several degrees across the active area, with corresponding variations in membrane hydration. The baseline for QH-DYNA is therefore a stack that is controlled for steady operation and that shows local temperature excursions during rapid current changes of the magnitude seen in the renewable supply profile.

The Company's stack test benches confirmed this baseline in early 2025. A stack operated on the steady-state control strategy showed an outlet temperature that tracked the load with a lag of approximately 40 seconds, and a spread of local temperatures across the active area that increased from around 2 °C at steady state to around 7 °C during the fastest ramp in the profile.

#### 5.15.3 Advance sought

The advance sought by QH-DYNA is a stack-level thermal management approach that holds temperature uniformity across the active area during rapid current changes without a significant increase in parasitic load. The Company's approach combines a predictive model of the stack thermal response with a coolant flow plan that is adjusted ahead of the expected current change, rather than in reaction to the outlet temperature.

The coolant flow plan is derived from a thermal model of the stack that predicts the local temperature response to a given change in current. The model is calibrated against the stack test bench data and is used to set the coolant flow for each ramp in the profile. The advance lies in using a predictive, stack-level plan for coolant flow during dynamic operation, which the baseline control strategy does not do.

The Company has not, in its review of the published literature, identified a published approach that uses a calibrated stack-level thermal model to set the coolant flow plan ahead of the current change for PEM stacks of the Company's design. The Company's view is that the approach is not obvious to a competent professional in the field, because the thermal model must capture the coupling between the electrochemical heat generation and the coolant flow in a stack whose heat generation varies across the active area.

#### 5.15.4 Uncertainties

Three uncertainties were identified for QH-DYNA at the start of the project in February 2025.

**Uncertainty 1: the thermal response under the fastest ramps.** The Company did not know whether a stack-level model calibrated at moderate ramp rates would predict the thermal response at the fastest ramps seen in the renewable supply profile, where the heat generation changes faster than the thermal mass of the stack can follow.

**Uncertainty 2: the effect of local excursions on membrane life.** The published literature on membrane degradation links membrane hydration cycles to mechanical fatigue, but the link has not been quantified for the local excursions produced by the Company's stack under dynamic load. It was not known whether the excursions that could be reduced by the coolant plan were large enough to change the membrane life materially.

**Uncertainty 3: the trade-off between coolant flow and parasitic power.** A coolant plan that holds temperature uniformity by increasing coolant flow increases the parasitic load of the stack, and the increase may offset the efficiency gain from the more uniform operation. The Company did not know at the start of the project where the optimum lay for its stack design.

#### 5.15.5 Methodology

The project used three elements. The first was a thermal model of the stack, developed by the Company in the first quarter of 2025 and calibrated against the test bench data from the steady-state tests. The model is a lumped-parameter representation of the stack, divided into sections across the active area, with each section having its own heat generation and heat transfer terms. The model was validated against the measured local temperatures at four points in the stack, instrumented with thermocouples during the test programme.

The second element was instrumented stack testing. The test programme applied the renewable supply profile to the stack at three ramp rates, and recorded the outlet temperature, the local temperatures at the four instrumented points, the coolant flow and the parasitic power at a sampling rate of one reading per second. Each test was repeated with the steady-state control strategy and with the predictive coolant plan, so that the difference between the two could be attributed to the plan.

The third element was iteration of the coolant flow plan. Each plan was derived from the model, tested on the stack, and compared with the measured local temperature spread and parasitic power. Plans that reduced the local spread but increased the parasitic power by more than the efficiency gain were rejected. The iterations were recorded in the project tickets with the model version and the test data reference.

Dr Joel Abernethy led the stack testing and the validation of the model against the thermocouple data. The coolant plan was developed by Dr Nadia Fenwick, who reviewed each iteration.

#### 5.15.6 Work log

The work log for QH-DYNA is set out below.

**February 2025.** The project was opened on 10 February (QHL-JR-2230). The first stack test on the steady-state control strategy was run on 24 February, confirming the baseline in section 5.15.2 (QHL-TD-0224).

**March 2025.** The first version of the thermal model was completed on 14 March. It was calibrated against the steady-state data and predicted the outlet temperature to within 0.6 °C at steady state. The first predictive coolant plan, designated CP-1, was derived from the model on 20 March and tested on the stack on 27 March (QHL-LB-0320, QHL-TD-0327).

**April 2025.** CP-1 reduced the local temperature spread during the slowest ramp, but at the fastest ramp the model under-predicted the local excursion by about 1.8 °C. The model was revised on 9 April to include the thermal mass of the coolant manifold, which had been omitted from the first version. The revised model was used to derive CP-2 on 16 April (QHL-LB-0417).

**May 2025.** CP-2 was tested on 6 and 12 May at the three ramp rates. The local temperature spread was reduced at all three ramp rates, but the parasitic power increased by a margin that was judged to offset part of the efficiency gain. The Company considered that the coolant flow ramp should be made less aggressive at the start of each ramp (QHL-TD-0512).

**June 2025.** CP-3 was derived on 3 June with a ramp-rate dependent coolant schedule. It was tested on 10 and 19 June. The parasitic power was reduced relative to CP-2, and the local spread at the fastest ramp was within the 3 °C figure that the Company had set as its working threshold for membrane fatigue. This threshold was taken from the Company's own accelerated membrane fatigue data and not from the published literature (QHL-TD-0619).

**July to September 2025.** The work was suspended for part of July while the stack test benches were used for QH-LOWIR. It resumed on 4 August with CP-4, a refinement of CP-3 in which the coolant schedule at the lowest ramp rate was simplified. CP-4 was tested on 11 and 18 August and on 2 September. The results were consistent with CP-3 and showed no further reduction in the parasitic power at the same local spread. The Company selected CP-4 as the plan for the remainder of the period on 9 September (QHL-JR-2265).

**October to December 2025.** The Company used CP-4 in a series of endurance runs on the stack, with the renewable supply profile applied for periods of 300 hours in total. The local temperature spread remained within the working threshold throughout. The endurance runs produced no evidence of performance change in the stack that could be attributed to the thermal regime, and the results were recorded in QHL-TD-1204. A final validation of the model against the endurance data was completed on 15 December (QHL-LB-1215).

#### 5.15.7 Outcomes

The principal outcome of QH-DYNA is a validated thermal model and a coolant flow plan, CP-4, that reduces the local temperature spread across the active area of the Company's stack during rapid current changes, without the parasitic power penalty of the steady-state approach at the fastest ramp. The Company has also established the local temperature threshold for membrane fatigue in its own design, which it had not previously been able to specify.

The uncertainty on the effect of local excursions on membrane life is not fully resolved. The endurance runs did not reach the membrane life targeted in the Company's specification, and the quantitative link between the excursions and membrane life remains to be established.
