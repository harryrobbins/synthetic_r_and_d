#### P1: EDITH: an engineering reasoning engine for verified calculation checking (GF-25-014)

**Main field of science or technology.** Computer science and information systems: software engineering and the verification of calculations. Civil and structural engineering: code compliance.

**Baseline.** Structural and hydraulic calculations are checked by a second engineer against the design code. The check is slow, depends on the reviewer's experience, and is applied unevenly across a large and growing volume of calculation work. No existing tool available to the company verified calculations against design codes with a documented reliability, and the published tools the company examined could not reliably interpret free-text calculation steps.

**Advance sought.** Development of a deterministic checker that parses a calculation into a symbolic representation and re-evaluates it against code-clause rules, including Eurocode-style load combinations. The advance is the proprietary engineering reasoning capability that results from this work, and the methods used to bring the assistant into the firm's calculation workflow.

**Technical uncertainties.** Whether free-text calculation steps, as written by engineers and by the approved assistant, can be canonicalised reliably enough for symbolic re-evaluation. Whether a rule set built from a small number of code clauses extends to the range of calculations in practice. Whether the checker's output can be trusted without a second manual check.

**How and when the uncertainties were resolved.** The parse rate was measured on successive calculation sets from April 2025. The rate reached 71% of steps parsed by November 2025, against a target of 90%. The uncertainty on canonicalisation is not yet fully resolved, and work to close the gap continues. The reliability of the rule evaluation was tested against pilot calculation sets in June and July 2025.

#### P2: Machine-learning surrogate models for real-time fluvial flood forecasting (GF-24-031)

**Main field of science or technology.** Environmental engineering: hydraulics and flood risk. Computer science: machine learning, graph neural networks and scientific computing.

**Baseline.** Two-dimensional hydrodynamic models of river and floodplain behaviour take between two and six hours to run for a single event. That is too slow for operational forecasting during a flood. Machine-learning surrogates published in the literature reproduce the outputs of such models, but they fail to generalise to catchments not seen in training, and they perform poorly where embankments overtop.

**Advance sought.** A physics-informed graph surrogate, trained on 4,100 synthetic events across six catchments, that reproduces water levels at the resolution of the hydrodynamic model. The target is a root mean square error under 0.15 m on unseen events, with run times under 40 seconds per event.

**Technical uncertainties.** Whether a surrogate trained on a set of catchments generalises to unseen catchments. Whether the surrogate can represent embankment overtopping, where the flow is discontinuous. Whether the error remains within the target on defended floodplains.

**How and when the uncertainties were resolved.** A convolutional surrogate was tested from April 2025 and failed on overtopping events. A graph-based surrogate was adopted in September 2025 and validated on unseen events from November 2025 to February 2026. The target was met in four of the six catchments. Work on the remaining catchments continues in the next programme phase.

#### P3: Two-phase immersion cooling heat rejection for high-density compute halls

**Main field of science or technology.** Mechanical engineering: heat transfer and two-phase flow. Building services engineering: data centre cooling.

**Baseline.** Published correlations for boiling heat-transfer coefficients in dielectric fluids were developed at lower heat fluxes than those now proposed for high-density compute halls. At elevated rack densities the correlations diverge, and the designer has no reliable basis for sizing the heat rejection plant at the design load.

**Advance sought.** Measured boiling heat-transfer coefficients for a dielectric fluid at elevated heat fluxes, from a purpose-built test rig, and a design approach that carries those coefficients into cooling strategy selection for high-density halls.

**Technical uncertainties.** Which of the available correlations, if any, represents the measured behaviour at elevated densities. Whether the coefficients measured at rig scale apply to a full-scale immersion tank. Whether the strategy approach can be applied across the range of design loads.

**How and when the uncertainties were resolved.** The rig was commissioned in April and May 2025. Measurements were taken on test sheets XC-IMM-01 to XC-IMM-22 between July 2025 and February 2026. The divergence between correlations was confirmed in December 2025. The full-scale question remains open and is noted as an uncertainty for future work.

#### P4: Common project data model and automated project set-up (Excelcion 2030 digital programme)

**Main field of science or technology.** Computer science and information systems: data engineering, data modelling and workflow automation.

### 5.1 P1: EDITH, an engineering reasoning engine for verified calculation checking

**Project reference and status.** Growth Fund paper GF-25-014. The project was registered with the Growth Fund in February 2025 and continued through the accounting period. Its competent professional is Dermot McGregor, Chief Digital and Information Officer. The project is led day to day by the Applied AI and Automation team, under the direction of the Head of Research and Innovation.

**Summary.** Structural and hydraulic calculations at Excelcion are produced by engineers, checked by a second engineer and issued. The check is the firm's primary control on calculation error. It is slow and it depends on the reviewer's experience and time. EDITH is the programme that set out to make the checking step more reliable and more consistent. It has two parts. The first is a deterministic checker that verifies a calculation against the clauses of the design code. The second is the rollout of an approved AI assistant to the first user cohort, with the training, guidance and support needed for engineers to use it on calculation work.

Excelcion has developed a proprietary engineering reasoning capability that verifies structural and hydraulic calculations against design codes with a reliability not previously achieved in the industry.

That capability is the subject of this project. The rest of this section sets out how it works, what was uncertain at the start, and what the work has shown.

### 5.2 Baseline

The baseline is the state of practice when the project began. Calculations for beams, columns, foundations, retaining structures, culverts, pipe networks and flood storage structures are produced in spreadsheets, in commercial design software, or by hand. Each calculation is checked by a second engineer who reads the steps, confirms that the load combinations and partial factors are correct, confirms that the clause references are current, and confirms that the arithmetic is right. The second check is an essential professional control. It is also the step most likely to be missed when a project is under time pressure.

Design-code checking tools were available at the start of the project. They confirmed the arithmetic of a standard design but did not interpret a free-text calculation. A calculation prepared in a spreadsheet or in a narrative report could not be fed into those tools without being rebuilt by hand. The published research on automated checking of structural calculations was limited, and the work that existed concentrated on closed-form design tables rather than on the free-text calculations that engineers write for real projects.

On that basis the baseline was taken to be a manual second check, applied unevenly, with no method for the firm to know how reliably it was catching errors across its project portfolio.

### 5.3 Advance sought

The advance sought was a method for checking calculations against design codes that worked on the calculations engineers actually produce. The method had to take a calculation written in ordinary language, convert it into a symbolic representation that a computer can evaluate, re-evaluate the load combinations and partial factors against the code clauses, and report every step where the stated result and the re-evaluated result disagree.

Three features distinguish the advance from the baseline. The first is that it operates on the calculation, not on a closed-form design table. The second is that it treats the code clause as a rule to be applied, not as a reference to be read. The third is that its output is traceable: each finding points to the step, the clause and the value that produced it. The advance does not replace the engineer's judgement. It is intended to make the second check more complete and more consistent, so that the engineer's time is spent on the questions that require judgement.

### 5.4 Technical uncertainties

At the start of the project the following uncertainties were identified in the project plan and recorded in GF-25-014.

**Uncertainty 1: canonicalisation of free-text steps.** A calculation step such as "Design moment at mid-span taken as 1.35 times permanent plus 1.5 times imposed, per 6.10" can be read by an engineer without difficulty. A parser has to recognise the variables, the units, the operators, the load classes and the clause reference, and then map them to a consistent symbolic form. It was not known at the outset whether a rule-based canonicaliser could reach a high enough parse rate across the range of notations used in practice, or whether the remaining steps would require manual rework that removed the benefit.

**Uncertainty 2: coverage of code rules.** A rule set built from a small number of code clauses would cover some calculations and not others. It was not known how many clauses would need to be encoded to cover the calculation types in the firm's portfolio, or whether the effort of encoding them would be proportionate to the benefit.

**Uncertainty 3: reliability of re-evaluation.** The checker's conclusions depend on the correct evaluation of load combinations and partial factors. An error in the evaluation, or a misreading of a clause, would produce a false finding. It was not known whether the evaluation could be made reliable enough to be relied on without a second manual check of every finding.

**Uncertainty 4: the role of the assistant.** From September 2025 the approved assistant was available to the first cohort for drafting and for checking support. It was not known whether a checker built on the firm's own rules could usefully review calculations drafted or edited with the assistant, or whether the assistant's output would introduce steps the rules could not interpret.

### 5.5 Methodology

The methodology has four stages. Each stage is a separate component, so that its performance can be measured on its own.

**Stage 1: capture.** A calculation is taken from the calculation record in the project file, either as a native spreadsheet or as a document, or from the output of the approved assistant when an engineer has used it to draft or edit a calculation. The capture step records the source, the version and the author of each step, so that every finding can be traced back to the original text.

**Stage 2: canonicalisation.** Each step is tokenised. Variables are matched to a symbol table built from the calculation's own definitions, and units are normalised to the SI system used in the design codes. Load classes (permanent, imposed, wind, snow, seismic and accidental) are recognised from the text and from the symbol table. Clause references are matched to the rule set. Operators are normalised, so that a multiplication written as a dot, a cross or a juxtaposition produces the same symbolic form. Where a step cannot be canonicalised, the component records a failure against the step and carries on with the rest of the calculation.

**Stage 3: structuring.** The canonicalised steps are assembled into an expression tree for the calculation. The tree records the dependencies between steps, so that a value used in a later step is traced back to the step that produced it. For load combinations, the component builds the combination from the load classes and the factors given in the clause, in a form equivalent to the fundamental combination at the ultimate limit state, with partial factors for permanent actions (gamma G) and variable actions (gamma Q), and combination factors (psi) where the clause requires them. Accidental and seismic combinations follow the same pattern with their own factors.

**Stage 4: re-evaluation and reporting.** The expression tree is evaluated numerically, with the same inputs as the original calculation. The component compares each stated result with the re-evaluated result within a tolerance set in the rule file, and reports every difference with the step, the clause, the stated value and the re-evaluated value. The report is designed to be read by the checking engineer, who decides what each finding means. The component does not change the calculation.

### 5.6 Rule set and design choices

The rule set is held in a versioned rule file. Each rule records the clause it implements, the load combination it applies to, the partial factors and the limits. The rule file is reviewed by a competent engineer before each release, and the change history is kept with the project record. The first release covered the load combinations and the beam and column checks most commonly used in the portfolio. Later releases extended coverage to foundations, retaining walls and the hydraulic checks used in pipe and culvert design.

The design choices were made to keep the checker's behaviour understandable. The component uses symbolic arithmetic rather than a learned model, so that each finding has a visible chain of reasoning. Where two clauses could apply, the rule file records the choice made and the reason for it. Where a calculation uses a value from outside the file, such as a material property from a supplier data sheet, the value is recorded as an input and its source is captured. The approach avoids any finding that the engineer cannot follow.

### 5.7 Technical core in detail

The technical core is the symbolic checker described in sections 5.5 and 5.6. It is the part of EDITH that carries the advance. The most difficult technical problem was canonicalisation, because a calculation is written for a human reader and not for a parser. Engineers abbreviate, reuse symbols across sections, state units inconsistently and refer to clauses by number without the table or edition. The canonicaliser addresses each of these in turn.

Symbol reuse is handled by scoping. A symbol defined in one section of a calculation is bound to that section, and a later reuse in a different section is treated as a new binding unless the text links it explicitly. Inconsistent units are handled by a unit table that recognises common forms, such as kN/m, kN per metre and kN.m-1, and converts them to a common base. Clause references are matched in two passes. The first pass matches the clause number to the rule set. The second pass checks the edition, where the calculation states one, and flags a mismatch where the rule set and the stated edition differ.

Load combinations are the most consequential part of the check. The fundamental combination at the ultimate limit state is built as the sum of the permanent and variable actions with their partial factors, and the leading variable action is identified from the calculation. The checker evaluates each combination the calculation uses and compares the result with the stated combination. Where a calculation uses an envelope of combinations, the checker evaluates each member of the envelope and reports the governing one. Where a calculation applies a combination factor that the clause does not require, the finding is recorded as an observation and not as an error, so that the engineer can decide whether the factor is conservative or simply unnecessary.

Hydraulic checks use the same pattern. A pipe capacity check, for example, is parsed into the hydraulic gradient, the roughness coefficient, the diameter, and the flow, and re-evaluated with the formula and the units given in the rule file. Where the calculation uses a tabulated coefficient, the table reference is recorded, and the value is checked against the table entry in the rule file rather than against a value typed into the calculation.

### 5.8 Parse performance

Parse performance is the measure of how many steps in a calculation are canonicalised. It is the best available indicator of whether the approach can be used across the firm's calculation work. The target was set at the start of the project at 90% of steps parsed. Performance was measured on successive calculation sets, each drawn from live projects, and each set was reviewed by a competent engineer to confirm the correct parse of a sample of steps.

On the first prototype, in April 2025, 48% of steps in a sample of structural calculations were parsed. The main failures were unrecognised notation and symbols defined in a separate document. ## 5. Technical narrative: P2 (flood forecasting surrogates), part 1 of 2, baseline, advance and method

### 5.14 P2 overview

**Project reference and status.** Growth Fund paper GF-24-031. The project is a continuation of the surrogate-model programme that began in 2024, and it was carried forward into the accounting period with the same technical plan, the same testbed and the same competent professional. The work was delivered jointly by Excelcion's Water and Data Science teams and by Tarnbeck Modelling Services Ltd, which provided modelling staff under a services agreement. The competent professional is Ian Ritchie, Principal Data Scientist.

**Summary.** Flood forecasting for a river catchment depends on a model of how water moves across the floodplain. The model used for the detailed work is a two-dimensional hydrodynamic model, which solves the equations of shallow water flow on a grid that covers the floodplain and the channel. Such a model gives an accurate picture of the flood, including the depth, the velocity and the extent of inundation, but it is slow. A single event takes between two and six hours to run. An operational forecast during a flood needs an answer in minutes, and the model cannot give one. P2 sets out to build a surrogate: a model that learns the behaviour of the hydrodynamic model from many runs, and reproduces its output for a new event in seconds.

### 5.15 Baseline

The baseline is the state of published and practised work at the start of the project. Two-dimensional hydrodynamic models are the accepted tool for detailed flood risk assessment in the United Kingdom. They are calibrated to observed events and used to test the effect of flood defences, storage schemes and land-use change. Their run time has been the main barrier to using them for real-time forecasting, and forecasters have relied on simpler one-dimensional models or on empirical relationships for that purpose.

Machine-learning surrogates have been proposed in the literature as a way to reduce run time. The published work reports good accuracy on the catchments used to train the surrogate. It also reports that the surrogates struggle to generalise. A surrogate trained on one set of catchments performs poorly on a catchment it has not seen, and the errors grow where the flow is discontinuous, for example where an embankment overtops and water moves across the defence in a short period. The defended floodplains that matter most for forecasting are the places where overtopping occurs, so the limitation is a serious one for operational use.

On that basis the baseline was taken to be a published surrogate that reproduces the hydrodynamic model accurately on training catchments, does not generalise to unseen catchments, and does not represent overtopping reliably.

### 5.16 Advance sought

The advance sought was a surrogate that generalises to unseen catchments and represents overtopping, that reproduces the water levels of the hydrodynamic model within a stated error, and that runs in under 40 seconds for a single event. The surrogate was to be physics-informed, meaning that the form of the model respects the conservation of mass across the floodplain, rather than learning the relationship between inputs and outputs without constraint.

The target error was a root mean square error under 0.15 m on unseen events, measured against the water level from the hydrodynamic model on the same grid. The target was set so that the forecast would be useful to the operational forecaster in the sense that the uncertainty introduced by the surrogate is smaller than the uncertainty already present in the rainfall forecast.

### 5.17 Technical uncertainties

The following uncertainties were recorded in GF-24-031 at the start of the period.

**Uncertainty 1: generalisation to unseen catchments.** It was not known whether a surrogate trained on six catchments would reproduce the hydrodynamic model accurately on a catchment it had not seen, or whether the surrogate would need to be retrained for each new catchment.

**Uncertainty 2: representation of overtopping.** Overtopping produces a sudden change in the flow across an embankment. Surrogates trained on smooth fields tend to smooth over that change. It was not known whether a surrogate could represent overtopping with an error comparable to the error on the rest of the floodplain, or whether overtopping events would need a separate treatment.

**Uncertainty 3: accuracy on defended floodplains.** Defended floodplains combine embankments, storage and channel constraints, and the errors in each component may compound. It was not known whether the error target could be met on defended floodplains without a separate model for each defence.

**Uncertainty 4: run time at operational scale.** The surrogate must run quickly on the hardware available to the forecasting service. It was not known whether the physics-informed architecture would stay within the run-time target once the grid resolution required for the forecast was reached.

### 5.18 Experimental design

The surrogate was trained on synthetic events. The events were generated by running the hydrodynamic model on a range of rainfall and river inflow scenarios for six catchments, chosen to represent the range of catchment size, slope, land use and defence arrangement in the company's flood portfolio. Each event produced a time series of water levels and velocities across the grid. In total 4,100 synthetic events were generated and used. The generation and the quality checks on each event are recorded in the experiment register, which runs from TMS-SUR-001 to TMS-SUR-140.

The synthetic events were split by catchment. For the generalisation test a leave-one-catchment-out design was used. For each of the six catchments in turn, a surrogate was trained on the other five and tested on the held-out catchment, so that every catchment was tested as an unseen catchment once. The events of the held-out catchment were not used at any stage of training or model selection for that test. This is the most demanding test of the approach, because it addresses the uncertainty identified in section 5.17 (1) directly.

Observed events were used for calibration checks only. Where an observed flood event was available for a catchment, the hydrodynamic model was calibrated to it, and the surrogate was compared against the calibrated model. The observed data were supplied by the Environment Agency and by the flood risk teams of the catchment operators, under the terms of the data licences recorded in the project file.

### 5.19 Architecture of the surrogate

The surrogate represents the floodplain as a graph. Each node is a computational cell of the grid, and each edge connects two neighbouring cells across a face. The node features include the ground elevation, the roughness, the defence height where a defence is present, and the water level and velocity at the previous time step. The edge features include the length of the shared face, the elevation difference and the flow capacity of the face, which in the case of an embankment depends on whether the crest is overtopped.

The model passes messages along the edges and updates the node states over a sequence of steps that corresponds to the time step of the hydrodynamic model. The update rule is constrained so that the net flow into each node balances the change in storage over the time step. This is the physics-informed element of the architecture. It means that the surrogate cannot create or destroy water, which is a property the hydrodynamic model has by construction and which a surrogate trained without the constraint does not reliably have.

Overtopping is represented by a separate flow law across the crest of the embankment, applied when the water level exceeds the crest height. The flow law is a standard weir relationship. The surrogate learns the coefficient and the transition from the training events, and the constraint on mass balance applies across the transition.

### 5.20 Methodology

The methodology is in four parts: generation of the training events; development of the surrogate architecture; training and validation; and testing on unseen catchments and on defended floodplains.

Training events were generated and quality checked as described in section 5.18. Each event was checked for numerical stability, mass conservation within the tolerance of the hydrodynamic model, and the presence of the expected flow pattern. Events that failed a check were rerun or removed, and the reason was recorded in the register.

The surrogate architecture was developed in stages. A convolutional surrogate was tested first, because it is the architecture most often used in the published work. A graph-based surrogate was developed second, with the constraint on mass balance and the overtopping law described in section 5.19. Both were compared on the validation catchments using the same metrics: root mean square error of water level, the error on the overtopping cells, and the run time for a single event on the forecasting hardware.

Training used the five training catchments in each fold. Model selection used a validation split drawn from those catchments. The held-out catchment in each fold was used only after the architecture had been selected. The test on defended floodplains used the events in which an embankment overtops, which were identified in the register and held back from training for the purpose of the test.

### 5.21 Competent professional's approach to the methodology

Ian Ritchie designed the methodology and reviewed each stage. His approach was to test the architecture on the hardest case first, which is the overtopping event, and to hold back the unseen catchments until the architecture had been selected. This sequencing is recorded in the experiment register. Where a result was unexpected, the architecture was revised and the revision recorded, rather than the test being rerun on a different split. The register records the iterations that failed as well as those that passed, and the failures are described in the log in section 5.22.

The quality of the synthetic training data is central to the outcome. The events were generated to span the range of conditions the forecasting service is expected to meet, and the hydrodynamic model used to generate them was calibrated to observed events on each training catchment before generation began. The calibration records are held in the project file.

### 5.22 Technical approach to run time

Run time is part of the advance. The hydrodynamic model takes between two and six hours per event. The surrogate must produce a comparable output in under 40 seconds on the forecasting hardware. The graph structure lends itself to parallel evaluation, and the architecture was designed so that the message-passing steps can be evaluated in batches on a single graphics processor. The run time was measured on the forecasting hardware at each stage of development. The measurements show that the final architecture runs a single event in between 28 and 39 seconds across the six catchments, which is inside the target. The run-time figures in the register use the same grid resolution as the operational forecast.

## 5. Technical narrative: P3 (two-phase immersion cooling heat rejection)

### 5.28 P3 overview

**Project reference and status.** P3 is carried out under the Growth Fund's dense-compute cooling programme, with the rig work recorded to the Keystone project code for the programme and the associated delivery codes for the mechanical engineering group. The competent professional is Kenneth Norris, Associate Director, Buildings Mechanical Engineering. The project was supported by Ewa Walsh, Group Technical Director, in her capacity as the firm's technical lead for building services.

**Summary.** High-density compute halls, such as those serving data processing and high-performance computing, produce a large heat load in a small area. The heat has to be removed continuously and the way it is removed determines the structure, plant space, energy use and resilience of the building. Two-phase immersion cooling, in which electronic equipment is submerged in a dielectric fluid that boils at the surface of the hot components, is one of the strategies being considered for the highest densities. The heat is removed by the vapour leaving the fluid, which is then condensed and returned. Whether this strategy can be sized reliably at the design load depends on the boiling heat-transfer coefficient of the fluid at the relevant heat flux.

### 5.29 Baseline

The baseline is the set of published correlations for boiling heat-transfer coefficients in dielectric fluids. These correlations were developed for lower heat fluxes than those now proposed for the highest-density halls, and they were calibrated against a limited set of fluids and surface conditions. The designer who must size the condenser and the heat rejection plant for a new hall has to choose a correlation. At the densities of interest the correlations give different answers, and there is no accepted basis on which to choose between them.

The consequence is that the heat rejection plant is sized conservatively, which carries a cost in capital and in energy use for the life of the building, or that it is sized on a correlation that may not represent the fluid at the relevant density. Neither outcome is satisfactory for a client who needs a reliable design.

### 5.30 Advance sought

The advance sought was a set of measured boiling heat-transfer coefficients for a dielectric fluid at elevated heat fluxes, obtained from a rig that reproduces the surface and fluid conditions of an immersion tank. The advance was intended to show which of the available correlations, if any, represents the measured behaviour at the relevant densities, and to give a design approach that carries the measured coefficients into the selection of a cooling strategy for a high-density hall.

### 5.31 Technical uncertainties

**Uncertainty 1: correlation fit at elevated densities.** It was not known which of the published correlations, if any, would represent the boiling heat-transfer coefficient at the highest heat fluxes considered for the halls.

**Uncertainty 2: scale.** A rig measures the coefficient on a heated surface of limited size. It was not known whether the coefficient measured at rig scale would apply to the larger surfaces and the flow conditions of a full-scale immersion tank.

**Uncertainty 3: design application.** It was not known whether a strategy approach based on the measured coefficients could be applied across the range of design loads that clients specify, or whether the coefficients would need to be measured again for each fluid.

### 5.32 Rig and methodology

The rig is a closed loop in which a dielectric fluid is held at saturation pressure and heated by an electrically heated test surface. The test surface is a copper block with embedded thermocouples, and the heat flux is controlled by the electrical input. The vapour generated at the surface rises to a condenser, where it is cooled and returned to the test chamber. The rig is instrumented for the surface temperature, the saturation temperature, the heat input, the condenser duty and the fluid pressure. Each measurement records the steady state reached at a given heat flux.

Commissioning took place in April and May 2025 in the dedicated laboratory at the Manchester office. Commissioning included calibration of the thermocouples, an energy balance check on the condenser and a repeatability test on the test surface. Repeatability was within the uncertainty stated for the instrumentation in the project file.

Measurements were taken on test sheets XC-IMM-01 to XC-IMM-22. Each test sheet records a sequence of heat flux steps, the steady state at each step, the coefficient derived from the surface temperature and the heat flux, and the uncertainty on that coefficient. The steps were taken at increasing heat flux, and a run was accepted only when the surface temperature had been steady for the period recorded on the sheet.

The coefficients were compared with the published correlations at each heat flux. The comparison was made for each correlation separately, and the divergence between correlations at the highest heat fluxes was recorded in the test sheet summary. The method is standard in the experimental literature on boiling and is a conventional approach to resolving uncertainty 1.

### 5.33 Application to cooling strategy

The measured coefficients were used to inform cooling strategy studies for high-density halls. The mechanical engineering group, which comprises nine full-time equivalent engineers, applied the coefficients to the condenser sizing and to the plant arrangement for the strategy options, following the firm's published guidance on the choice of cooling strategy for dense computing. The guidance is set out in the Insights article Cooling dense computing: choose the strategy before the building, published on 10 June 2025 by Kenneth Norris. The article describes the decision points the group uses to select a strategy, including the design heat load, the range of loads and the plant space each strategy requires.

Design studies for clients' data-centre commissions made use of the rig results in this way. The studies were delivered under the appointments in place with those clients and billed under the terms of those appointments. The time the group spent on those studies is recorded to the P3 Keystone code in proportion to the research content of the work, and the averages are set out in section 6.

The application of the coefficients to strategy selection is part of P3 because it tests uncertainty 3 directly. The studies showed that the measured coefficients change the condenser sizing at the highest densities, which is the outcome the advance was designed to test.

## 5. Technical narrative: P4 (common project data model and automated project set-up)

### 5.38 P4 overview

**Project reference and status.** P4 is carried out under the Excelcion 2030 digital programme. Its work is recorded to the Keystone project codes for the programme and to the Lighthouse delivery codes. The competent professional is Dermot McGregor, Chief Digital and Information Officer, who sponsors the programme.

**Summary.** Excelcion 2030 sets out the firm's ambition to deliver better and safer engineering, and to make its working practices more effective across the group. Digital transformation is one of its main strands. A large part of a project's early effort is spent establishing its records: the client, the programme, the cost codes, the design data, the document structure and the links between them. At present that work is carried out largely by hand, in Keystone and in the project file, and the structure of the records differs between projects and between business units. P4 sets out to create a common project data model for the firm, and to automate the set-up of a new project from that model across Keystone and Lighthouse.

### 5.39 Baseline

The baseline is the firm's practice in 2025. A new project is set up in Keystone by a project administrator, who creates the project record, assigns the cost codes, enters the client and programme data and links the project to the document structure. Design data is held in Lighthouse for analysis and in the project file for issue. Legacy project records from before the Keystone implementation are held in Ledgerline, which has been read-only since April 2021, and some of those records are still needed for live projects that began before the migration.

The set-up process takes time and introduces error. Records are entered more than once, the field definitions differ between business units, and a project that uses legacy data must be reconciled by hand. The firm's view is that automated set-up on a common model would reduce both the time and the error. It would also allow the data in Lighthouse to be used consistently across the group.

### 5.40 Advance sought

The advance sought was a common project data model for the firm, which defines the entities, relationships and field semantics of a project record in a single form, and a method for populating a new project from that model automatically across Keystone and Lighthouse. The model is intended to accept data from legacy records, so that the history of a project can be carried forward into the new structure without manual re-entry.

### 5.41 Technical uncertainties

**Uncertainty 1: integration of legacy data.** The central uncertainty is integrating heterogeneous legacy project data from Ledgerline and Keystone at enterprise scale. The project records were created over many years under different conventions. It was not known whether the records could be integrated into a single model without loss of meaning, or whether the mapping would need to be made by hand for each record type.

**Uncertainty 2: automation across project types.** The firm delivers a wide range of projects, from small design commissions to multi-year programmes. It was not known whether an automated set-up process could be relied on across that range, or whether it would need a separate procedure for each type of project.

**Uncertainty 3: scale.** Excelcion's records span many thousands of projects. It was not known whether the integration approach would work at enterprise scale, which is the level at which the model must operate to be useful.

### 5.42 Methodology

The methodology has three parts: analysis of the legacy data, design of the common model, and testing of the automated set-up.

The legacy data analysis examined the record types held in Ledgerline and Keystone, and the fields and codes used in each. The analysis produced a catalogue of fourteen record types, with the field definitions, the value sets and the relationships between them. Each record type was mapped to a candidate entity in the common model. Mappings were tested against a sample of records from each source, and the results were reviewed by the Digital Delivery team with the business units that own each record type.

The common model was designed as a set of entities and relationships, with each field given a single definition and a permitted set of values. The model is held in a versioned schema in Lighthouse. The first version was released in June 2025 and was tested against legacy data from the Ledgerline and Keystone sources. Later versions extended the model to cover design data and programme records.

The automated set-up was tested in three business units from January 2026. A project was set up by the automated process, and the result was compared with the record set up by hand for the same project. The comparison was recorded for each field, and each difference was reviewed with the business unit concerned.

### 5.43 Technical approach to integration

The integration of legacy data uses a set of transformation rules that map each source field to the common model. Where a source field maps to a single model field, the rule is a direct mapping with a value translation. Where a source field must be split or combined, the rule records the logic used. Where a source record has no equivalent in the model, the record is held in an archive table with its source reference, so that it can be retrieved if needed. The rules are versioned, and each change is reviewed by the Digital Delivery team.

The integration relies on the consistency of identifiers across the sources. Project identifiers are reconciled using the client, the programme and the start date, with a manual review step for ambiguous matches. The manual review is necessary because the same project may appear under different identifiers in the two systems, and the reconciliation is one of the main sources of uncertainty in the work.
